Jeremiah 38 & 39 — Rescued From the Cistern as Jerusalem Falls

Study note · compiled Sep 22, 2026 · Bible reading for the week of Sep 28–Oct 04, 2026 · Treasures theme: "Continue Supporting One Another"

"For I will surely provide you with escape, and you will not fall by the sword. You will have your life as a spoil, because you trusted in me."
Key text — Jeremiah 39:18

Focus: The last months of the siege. The princes demand Jeremiah's death, a weak king hands him over, and he is let down into a cistern of mud to die. The man who saves him is not a priest or a prince but a foreign court official, Ebed-melech the Ethiopian. Zedekiah, meanwhile, hears Jehovah's way out one final time and cannot bring himself to take it. Chapter 39 records the result: the wall is breached, the king is caught, his sons are killed before his eyes, the city burns — and both Jeremiah and Ebed-melech come through alive, exactly as promised. The note follows five study questions: the setting; what Jeremiah was doing and how he coped; what the account tells us about Jehovah; how we can imitate Jehovah's qualities; and other ways the account benefits us.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When. Chapter 38 falls in the closing stage of the siege, when "there is no bread left in the city" (38:9) — about 608-607 B.C.E. Chapter 39 is dated precisely: the siege began "in the ninth year of King Zedekiah… in the tenth month" (39:1), and the wall was breached "in the 11th year of Zedekiah, in the fourth month, on the ninth day" (39:2) — 607 B.C.E. The message to Ebed-melech (39:15-18) was given earlier, while Jeremiah was still confined in the Courtyard of the Guard.

607 B.C.E. — Jeremiah is released on Nebuchadnezzar's orders and entrusted to Gedaliah (39:11-14); Ebed-melech's life is spared, as promised (39:17, 18).

What was going on. Jeremiah kept telling the people that anyone who stayed in the city would die, but "the one who surrenders to the Chaldeans will keep living" (38:2). The princes said he was "weakening the morale of the soldiers" and demanded his death (38:4). Zedekiah gave in: "He is in your hands, for the king cannot do anything to stop you" (38:5). They lowered Jeremiah into a cistern that had "no water… only mud" (38:6). Ebed-melech went straight to the king at the Gate of Benjamin, obtained permission, and with 30 men drew Jeremiah out, padding the ropes with worn-out rags (38:7-13). Zedekiah then met Jeremiah secretly and heard Jehovah's terms once more (38:14-23) — but he was more afraid of people than of Jehovah (38:19, 24-26). Chapter 39 shows the outcome.

2.What was Jeremiah doing — and how did he cope?

What he was doing. Still preaching Jehovah's message of surrender to the people (38:1-3); surviving the cistern (38:6-13); counselling the king one last time (38:14-23); and, though a prisoner himself, carrying Jehovah's comforting promise to the man who had rescued him (39:15-18).

How he coped.

  • He kept speaking the truth under a death threat. He knew the risk — "If I tell you, you will certainly put me to death" (38:15) — and spoke anyway once the king swore to protect him (38:16, 17).
  • He accepted help humbly and cooperated with his rescuers. Told to put the rags under his armpits, "Jeremiah did so" (38:12).
  • He pleaded kindly with the king. "Obey, please, the voice of Jehovah in what I am telling you, and it will go well with you, and you will continue to live" (38:20).
  • He used discretion. When the princes questioned him, he told them what the king had instructed and no more (38:24-27).
  • He thought of his friend. "When Jeremiah learned about it, he did not remain silent, hoping that his friend would somehow cope on his own. Although Jeremiah was imprisoned, he did what he could by sharing Jehovah's comforting promise with his friend Ebed-melech." (w19.11 6 ¶17)

3.What does this account tell me about Jehovah?

  • He is merciful to the very end. With the city about to fall, he still offered Zedekiah life for himself and his family: "If you surrender… your life will be spared, and this city will not be burned with fire, and you and your household will be spared" (38:17). Even his command was phrased as a plea: "Obey, please, the voice of Jehovah… and it will go well with you" (38:20).
  • He respects our freedom to choose. Jehovah set out both paths plainly, with the outcome of each (38:17, 18, 20-23), and then let Zedekiah decide. He does not force obedience, but he makes sure no one can say they were not warned.
  • He is impartial. The man he singled out for rescue was a foreigner, "Ebed-melech the Ethiopian" (39:16), not one of Judah's princes or priests. What mattered to Jehovah was not nationality or rank, but the man's heart: "because you trusted in me" (39:18).
  • He notices and rewards trust and courage. Ebed-melech risked his position to save Jeremiah (38:7-10). Jehovah did not let that go unnoticed; he sent a personal message promising, "I will surely provide you with escape" (39:18). "Godly courage is rewarded." (w13 1/15 9 ¶12)
  • He understands our fears. Jehovah spoke to what Ebed-melech was actually afraid of: "You will not be handed over to the men you fear" (39:17). He knows the specific fears of each of his servants and answers them.
  • He is faithful to every promise — of judgment and of rescue. The city was breached, the king's house and the people's houses burned, and the walls torn down, exactly as foretold (39:1-8). The two men he promised to save came through alive (39:11-14, 16-18). Both halves of his word came true.
  • He is in control, even through unlikely means. He rescued Jeremiah from the cistern through a court official (38:10-13), and later protected him through the orders of Babylon's king himself: "Take him and look after him; do him no harm, and grant whatever he asks of you" (39:12).

For meditation: What does it mean to me that Jehovah sent a personal message to one frightened man in a doomed city?

4.How can I imitate Jehovah's qualities?

  • Keep giving people the opportunity to do right. Jehovah offered Zedekiah a way out even at the last hour (38:17). We imitate his mercy when we keep the door open for someone who has made poor choices, rather than decide that they are beyond help.
  • Encourage with kindness, not pressure. Jehovah's message came with "Obey, please" (38:20). When we urge a family member, a Bible student or a fellow Christian to follow Bible counsel, a kind and respectful tone reflects him.
  • Respect the choices of others. Jehovah explained the consequences plainly and then let Zedekiah decide (38:17, 18). Parents of adult children, elders and teachers can explain clearly what the Bible says and what may follow, and then respect the person's right to choose.
  • Welcome people of every background. Jehovah valued an Ethiopian official as highly as any Israelite (39:16-18). We imitate his impartiality by showing the same warmth to everyone, whatever their nationality, race or status.
  • Notice and commend courage and faith in others. Jehovah told Ebed-melech exactly why he was being rewarded: "because you trusted in me" (39:18). Specific commendation lets people know that what they did was seen and valued.
  • Comfort people in their particular fears. Jehovah addressed "the men you fear" (39:17), not fears in general. When we comfort someone, we can first listen to learn what is actually troubling them, then share a Bible promise that fits.

5.What other ways can this account benefit me?

  • Speak up for others as Ebed-melech did. "Despite the danger in which it placed him because of the existing hatred for Jeremiah, Ebed-melech appealed to Zedekiah in behalf of the prophet." (w13 1/15 9 ¶12) The workbook asks: "Whom in my congregation might I need to speak up for?" — for example, "At times, sisters may need someone to speak up for them when they face a specific challenge." (w20.09 24 ¶15)
  • Comfort others as Jeremiah did. Even when our own situation is hard, we can pass on Jehovah's promises to a friend who is afraid (39:15-18).
  • Encourage others to obey Jehovah. Jeremiah's "Obey, please" (38:20) was urgent and kind at once.
  • Do not let fear of people outweigh obedience to Jehovah. Zedekiah said, "I am afraid of the Jews who have deserted to the Chaldeans" (38:19). The foreigner Ebed-melech, who trusted Jehovah, was the one who survived (39:18).
  • Practical kindness counts. The rags between the ropes and Jeremiah's armpits were a small, thoughtful detail in a rescue carried out in a hurry (38:11, 12).
  • A family head's choices reach the whole household. See the Closer look below.

Closer look — Zedekiah's choice (Spiritual Gems)

The question: Jer 39:6, 7 — What bad choice did King Zedekiah make, and what can family heads learn from his example?

Jeremiah had told the king that if he surrendered, "you and your household will be spared" (38:17). He refused. "The king's bad choice affected him, his wives, and his sons. He watched his sons be killed; then he was blinded and taken captive to Babylon." (jr 92 ¶1; 38:18-23; 39:6, 7) jr contrasts him with Joshua, who said, "As for me and my household, we shall serve Jehovah" (Josh. 24:15). The lesson for family heads: decisions about obeying Jehovah are never private; the household lives with the result.

Were these men real? Two clay seal impressions (bullae) found in the City of David, in a layer dating to Jerusalem's destruction in 607 B.C.E., bear the names "Jucal the son of Shelemiah" and "Gedaliah the son of Pashhur" — two of the princes named at 38:1 who wanted Jeremiah dead. "Yes, those named in Jeremiah chapter 38 were real people." (jr chap. 5, box)

Key passages (New World Translation)

Jeremiah 38:4-6

“The princes said to the king: “Please have this man put to death, for this is how he is weakening the morale of the soldiers who are left in this city, as well as that of all the people, by speaking such words to them. For this man seeks, not the peace of this people, but their calamity.” King Zedekiah replied: “Look! He is in your hands, for the king cannot do anything to stop you.” So they took Jeremiah and threw him into the cistern of Malchijah the son of the king, which was in the Courtyard of the Guard. They let Jeremiah down by ropes. Now there was no water in the cistern, only mud, and Jeremiah began to sink down into the mud.”

Jeremiah 38:9, 10

“O my lord the king, what these men have done to Jeremiah the prophet is evil! They have thrown him into the cistern, and he will die there because of the famine, for there is no bread left in the city.” Then the king commanded Ebed-melech the Ethiopian: “Take 30 men with you from here, and pull Jeremiah the prophet up out of the cistern before he dies.”

Jeremiah 38:17

“Jeremiah then said to Zedekiah: “This is what Jehovah, the God of armies, the God of Israel, says, ‘If you surrender to the princes of the king of Babylon, your life will be spared, and this city will not be burned with fire, and you and your household will be spared.”

Jeremiah 38:20

“But Jeremiah said: “You will not be handed over to them. Obey, please, the voice of Jehovah in what I am telling you, and it will go well with you, and you will continue to live.”

Jeremiah 39:6, 7

“The king of Babylon had the sons of Zedekiah slaughtered before his eyes there at Riblah, and the king of Babylon had all the nobles of Judah slaughtered. Then he blinded the eyes of Zedekiah, after which he bound him with copper fetters to bring him to Babylon.”

Jeremiah 39:11, 12

“Now King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon gave Nebuzaradan the chief of the guard these orders concerning Jeremiah: “Take him and look after him; do him no harm, and grant whatever he asks of you.””

Jeremiah 39:16-18

“Go and tell Ebed-melech the Ethiopian, ‘This is what Jehovah of armies, the God of Israel, says: “Here I am fulfilling my words on this city for calamity and not for good, and in that day you will see it happen.”’ “‘But I will rescue you in that day,’ declares Jehovah, ‘and you will not be handed over to the men you fear.’ “‘For I will surely provide you with escape, and you will not fall by the sword. You will have your life as a spoil, because you trusted in me,’ declares Jehovah.”
