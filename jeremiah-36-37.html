Jeremiah 36 & 37 — The Burned Scroll and the Prophet in Prison

Study note · compiled Sep 22, 2026 · Bible reading for the week of Sep 21-27, 2026 · Treasures theme: "Jehovah Supports Those Who Support His Kingdom"

"Perhaps when those of the house of Judah hear of all the calamity that I intend to bring on them, they may turn back from their evil ways, so that I may forgive their error and their sin."
Key text — Jeremiah 36:3

Focus: Chapter 36 shows what one king did with God's word: he listened to it column by column, cut each piece off with a knife and fed it to the fire. Jehovah's answer was simply to have it written again — with more added. Chapter 37 moves forward some fifteen years to the siege under Zedekiah: Jeremiah is falsely accused of deserting, beaten and thrown into a dungeon, yet he still tells the king the truth, and Jehovah sees that he gets bread every day. The note follows five study questions: the setting; what Jeremiah was doing and how he coped; what the account tells us about Jehovah; how we can imitate Jehovah's qualities; and other ways the account benefits us.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When. Chapter 36 opens "in the fourth year of Jehoiakim" (36:1) — 625 B.C.E., also the first year of Nebuchadnezzar (25:1). The public reading and the burning of the scroll followed in "the fifth year… in the ninth month," in winter, with the king beside a brazier (36:9, 22). Chapter 37 belongs to the reign of Zedekiah (37:1), during the final siege, when an Egyptian army's approach made the Babylonians withdraw for a while (37:5, 11) — about 609-608 B.C.E.

What was going on. In chapter 36 Jeremiah was barred from the temple ("I am confined and unable to enter the house of Jehovah," 36:5), so Baruch read the scroll aloud there on a public fast day (36:6, 9, 10). The princes took it seriously, warned Baruch and Jeremiah to hide, and reported to the king (36:16-20). Jehoiakim's response was contempt: "they felt no dread," and the scroll went into the fire piece by piece (36:23, 24). In chapter 37 Judah was split between those who would submit to Babylon and those who looked to Egypt. When the Chaldeans lifted the siege, Jeremiah set out for his home territory in Benjamin, was seized at the city gate on a charge of desertion, beaten and held "many days" in the dungeon of the house of Jehonathan the secretary (37:12-16). Insight says of this place: "The house likely had subterranean quarters suitable for imprisonment" (it "Jehonathan" No. 2), and "Conditions were so bad there that Jeremiah feared for his life." (it "Prison")

2.What was Jeremiah doing — and how did he cope?

What he was doing. In chapter 36 he was dictating: "Jeremiah thus dictated to his secretary, Baruch, all that God had told him in the preceding 23 years." (jr chap. 2 ¶14) When that scroll was burned, he dictated it all again (36:32). In chapter 37 he was delivering an unpopular message — the Chaldeans will return — and then enduring prison for it. "The princes of Judah claimed that Jeremiah was throwing in his lot with the Babylonians." (jr 28 ¶23)

How he coped.

  • He passed on Jehovah's correction to his discouraged helper. While Baruch was writing the scroll "in the fourth year of Jehoiakim," he complained, "Woe to me, for Jehovah has added grief to my pain! I am weary from my groaning, and I have found no resting-place." Jeremiah delivered Jehovah's answer: "But you are seeking great things for yourself. Stop seeking such things," with the promise, "I will grant you your life as a spoil" (45:1, 3, 5). "Baruch accepted the correction and continued to enjoy Jehovah's approval." (w25.07 22 ¶8) A year later he read the scroll in the temple and faced the princes (36:8-18).
  • He found another way when he could not go himself. Barred from the temple, he sent Baruch (36:5, 6). The work did not stop because the worker was restricted.
  • He started again without complaint. Years of messages went into the fire; the record says only that he "took another scroll" and dictated them again (36:32).
  • He stated the truth calmly when accused. "It is not true! I am not deserting to the Chaldeans" (37:14).
  • He would not soften Jehovah's message, even to get out of prison. Asked in secret, "Is there any word from Jehovah?" he answered, "There is!… You will be given into the hand of the king of Babylon!" (37:17). As jr says, "Jeremiah would still not soften Jehovah's message." (jr 28 ¶23)
  • He used a respectful, lawful appeal. He asked the king why he was imprisoned and requested, "Do not send me back to the house of Jehonathan the secretary, or I will die there" (37:18-20). The request was granted (37:21).

3.What does this account tell me about Jehovah?

  • He is patient. The scroll held everything he had spoken "from the first day I spoke to you in the days of Josiah to this day" (36:2) — more than two decades of warnings to the same people. He did not give up on them after the first refusal, or the tenth.
  • He longs to forgive. His stated reason for the whole project was mercy: "Perhaps… they may turn back from their evil ways, so that I may forgive their error and their sin" (36:3; compare 36:7). The word "perhaps" shows a God who hopes for the best from people, even from a rebellious nation.
  • He is compassionate toward his discouraged servants. When Baruch said, "I am weary from my groaning, and I have found no resting-place," Jehovah heard it and answered him personally, by name (45:2, 3). His correction was direct — "Stop seeking such things" — yet it came with a promise: "I will grant you your life as a spoil" (45:5). He corrects in order to keep, not to crush.
  • He is just and impartial. Rank gave Jehoiakim no protection: "I will call him and his descendants and his servants to account for their error" (36:31). Jehoiakim "will have no one to sit on the throne of David, and his dead body will be left exposed to the heat by day and the frost by night" (36:30). Both parts came true. Jehoiakim died during the Babylonian siege of 618 B.C.E. before he could be taken away in fetters, and his body was denied a decent burial; his son Jehoiachin reigned only three months and ten days before surrendering. The details are in the Closer look below.
  • He is truthful, and his word cannot be destroyed. Burning the scroll changed nothing: "Take another scroll and write on it all the same words that were on the first scroll" (36:28), and "many more words like those were added" (36:32). He also refused to let Zedekiah live on false hope: "Do not deceive yourselves… Even if you were to strike down the entire army of the Chaldeans… they would still rise up from their tents and burn this city with fire" (37:9, 10).
  • He is protective. When the king ordered their arrest, "Jehovah kept them concealed" (36:26). He knows how to shield those who are doing his work.
  • He cares about everyday needs. In a city running out of food, Jeremiah received "a round loaf of bread daily" until "all the bread in the city was gone" (37:21). "Still, Jeremiah trusted not in men but in God, who promised to care for him." (w08 10/15 11 ¶18)

For meditation: Which of these qualities do I most need to remember right now — his patience with me, his readiness to forgive, or his care for my daily needs?

4.How can I imitate Jehovah's qualities?

  • Be patient with people who are slow to respond. Jehovah kept speaking to Judah for more than twenty years (36:2). A relative, a householder or a Bible student may need many conversations before the truth reaches the heart.
  • Hope the best for others, and be ready to forgive. Jehovah's "Perhaps… so that I may forgive" (36:3) shows a heart that looks for the opening, not the excuse. We imitate him when we do not write people off and when we forgive readily.
  • Help the discouraged with honesty and hope. Jehovah listened to Baruch's exact words, corrected his thinking and gave him a reason to keep going (45:3-5). Elders, parents and friends can do the same: listen first, then give kind, honest counsel that leaves the person with hope.
  • Be truthful, even when the truth is unwelcome. Jehovah did not flatter Zedekiah with false hope (37:9), and Jeremiah told the king, "There is!… You will be given into the hand of the king of Babylon!" (37:17). Real love tells the truth, tactfully.
  • Protect and provide for those under pressure. Jehovah concealed his servants (36:26) and fed his prophet daily (37:21); the princes warned, "Go and hide yourselves, you and Jeremiah" (36:19). We imitate that care by looking out for brothers and sisters facing opposition, hardship or disaster.
  • Treat everyone impartially. Jehovah judged a king by the same standard as anyone else (36:31). We imitate him when we do not give special treatment to those with position or wealth.

5.What other ways can this account benefit me?

  • Jehovah's message will outlast its opponents. A king could burn a scroll, but he could not burn the word; it came back longer (36:28, 32). Opposition to the good news today will end the same way.
  • How I respond to God's word matters. The princes "looked at one another in dread" and acted (36:16); the king "felt no dread" (36:24). The same message produced opposite hearts.
  • Stay neutral, even when misunderstood. In Jeremiah's day, "Judah was now split into opposing factions—those who favored submission to Babylon and those who urged rebellion." (jr 27 ¶22) Jeremiah belonged to neither party; he simply delivered Jehovah's message that the Chaldeans would return and take the city (37:8-10). Because of that message, the guard officer Irijah took him for a traitor: "You are deserting to the Chaldeans!" Jeremiah answered calmly, "It is not true! I am not deserting to the Chaldeans" (37:13, 14), and he accepted a beating and imprisonment rather than change the message or take a side (37:15, 16). "How often Jehovah's people in modern times have faced perils because of their conscientious refusal to get involved in political controversies!" (jr 28 ¶23) The workbook asks: "How can the examples of Jeremiah and others motivate me to support God's Kingdom during times of political unrest?"
  • Jehovah supports those who support his Kingdom. A daily loaf in a starving city was his care made visible (37:21). He knows how to provide for his servants under pressure.
  • Discouragement can be corrected before it becomes a fall. Baruch's low point came at the start of the scroll project, not after the burning (45:1). Accepting counsel early left him ready for the harder tests that followed (36:14-19, 32).
  • Loyal helpers make the work possible. Baruch wrote, read and hid at personal risk (36:8, 18, 19). Supporting others in the ministry is part of Kingdom support.
  • Respectful use of lawful means is appropriate. Jeremiah pleaded his case to the king (37:18-20) — much as God's people today may appeal to authorities for relief.

Closer look — how Jehoiakim's sentence came true (Spiritual Gems)

The question: Jer 36:30 — How did the prophecy that Jehoiakim would have no successor come true?

Insight explains that after Jehoiakim's son Jehoiachin surrendered, "Nebuchadnezzar elevated Jehoiachin's uncle Zedekiah to the throne of Judah. (2Ch 36:9, 10) This fulfilled Jeremiah's prophecy that Jehoiakim would have no one sitting on the throne of David. (Jer 36:30) Jehoiakim's son Jehoiachin ruled a mere three months and ten days." (it "Jehoiakim" ¶9) A reign that short, ending in exile, was no real succession; the throne passed to Jehoiakim's brother, not to his line.

"His dead body will be left exposed." The second half of 36:30 matches an earlier prophecy: "They will not mourn him… With the burial of a donkey he will be buried, Dragged about and thrown away, Outside the gates of Jerusalem" (22:18, 19). "The ass, at death, was simply dragged unceremoniously outside the city and thrown on the refuse heap." (it "Ass")

  • He died under siege. Nebuchadnezzar "came up against him in order to bind him with two copper fetters to take him off to Babylon" (2 Ch. 36:6), yet it was his son who finally surrendered (2 Ki. 24:10-12). "It therefore appears that Jehoiakim died during the siege of the city, perhaps in the early part thereof." The fetters "were not used as planned." (it "Jehoiakim")
  • His body was not honored. "Jehovah's prophecy through Jeremiah (22:18, 19; 36:30) indicated that Jehoiakim was not to receive a decent burial; his corpse was to lie unattended outside the gates of Jerusalem, exposed to the sun's heat by day and the frost by night." (it "Jehoiakim")
  • The details are not recorded. "The Bible does not specify how he died or what was done with his corpse, but it does show that his son Jehoiachin succeeded him during the siege." (jr, "fate of four Judean kings" ¶10) A tradition preserved by Josephus says Nebuchadnezzar killed him and had his body thrown outside the walls, but "there is no way to confirm the Jewish tradition." (it "Jehoiakim")
  • What about 2 Kings 24:6? "Then Jehoiakim was laid to rest with his forefathers" does not have to mean an honorable burial. The same words are used of Ahab (1 Ki. 22:40), whose blood the dogs licked up after he died in battle (1 Ki. 22:37, 38). Inference: the expression records a king's death in the royal annals rather than describing his burial, so it does not contradict Jeremiah 22:19 or 36:30.
