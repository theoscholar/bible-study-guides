Account overview

Jeremiah 51 is Jehovah’s judgment on Babylon, spoken before Babylon fell. Jeremiah 52 is the historical account of Jerusalem’s fall and of a release that closes the book of Jeremiah on a note of kindness.

Chapter 51 (64 verses) tells Babylon to expect a “destructive wind” (51:1), warns God’s people, “Flee out of the midst of Babylon” (51:6), contrasts Jehovah the Maker with lifeless idols (51:15-19), summons the Medes and other nations against Babylon (51:11, 27-28), and ends with an acted prophecy: in the fourth year of Zedekiah, Jeremiah gave the written words against Babylon to Seraiah the quartermaster, who was to read them aloud at Babylon and throw the scroll, tied to a stone, into the Euphrates (51:59-64).

Chapter 52 (34 verses) records Zedekiah’s 11-year reign, his rebellion, the siege and fall of Jerusalem in 607 B.C.E., the capture, blinding and imprisonment of Zedekiah, the burning of the temple and the city, the exile of 4,600 people in three deportations, and — last of all — the release of the exiled King Jehoiachin by Evil-merodach in 581 B.C.E. (52:31-34).

Keep the order straight. Jeremiah 51:64 says, “Down to this point are the words of Jeremiah.” Chapter 52 is therefore a historical appendix, largely parallel to 2 Kings 24:18–25:30. The prophecy in chapter 51 was spoken earlier still (the scroll episode, 614 B.C.E., 51:59), the events of chapter 52:1-30 happened in 607 B.C.E., Jehoiachin was released in 581 B.C.E., and Babylon itself did not fall until October 5, 539 B.C.E. — after the book of Jeremiah was completed in 580 B.C.E.

Connection to the Bible’s theme

The Bible’s theme is the vindication of Jehovah’s sovereignty by means of his Kingdom. These two chapters show both sides of that vindication: Jehovah executes judgment on an unrepentant nation that destroyed his temple, and he keeps his promises to his own people.

Jeremiah’s commission itself states the two sides. God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 14, ¶1, p. 168:

God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 14, ¶1, p. 168

“JEHOVAH gave Jeremiah a twofold commission. One part was ‘to uproot and to pull down and to destroy and to tear down.’ The other was ‘to build and to plant.’ The prophet accomplished the former by exposing the wickedness of the proud Jews, pronouncing God’s judgment on them as well as on Babylon. Yet, Jeremiah’s prophecies included hope for the future. He foretold the building of what God purposed to be built and the planting of what He purposed to be planted. For example, Jeremiah was fulfilling the second part of his assignment when he directed attention to the restoration of the Jews to their homeland.—Jer. 1:10; 30:17, 18.”

In chapter 51 Jehovah is the Repurchaser who pleads his people’s legal case (51:36; compare 50:34). In chapter 52 he lets Jerusalem fall because of the nation’s unfaithfulness, yet he does not abandon his purpose: the 70 years of servitude end, the exiles return (Jer. 25:11, 12; 29:10; Ezra 1:1-3), and the book closes with kindness shown to Jehoiachin (52:31-34).

God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 13, ¶19, pp. 163-164, states the point of the fulfilled prophecies in Jeremiah:

God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 13, ¶19, pp. 163-164

“As you can see, chapter after chapter of the book of Jeremiah is packed with prophecies concerning individuals and nations. The majority of these prophecies have already been fulfilled. This reality alone makes the book worth your attention and study because it confirms for you something about your great God. Jehovah has done what he had in mind, and he will yet do so. (Read Isaiah 46:9-11.) This can strengthen your confidence in what the Bible foretells. …”

That is why the warning in chapter 51 still matters: the same Jehovah who did what he had in mind against ancient Babylon will do what he has in mind against “Babylon the Great,” the world empire of false religion (Rev. 14:8; 17:1, 2, 5; Jer. 50:2; 51:8 — jr 165 ¶21).

What to know before reading?

When

Chapter 51 was spoken before Babylon fell. Its scroll was written and sent to Babylon in 614 B.C.E., in the fourth year of Zedekiah (51:59-64) — 75 years before Babylon fell on October 5, 539 B.C.E. Chapter 52 records events that happened in between: the siege and fall of Jerusalem in 607 B.C.E., and the release of Jehoiachin in 581 B.C.E. The book of Jeremiah was completed in 580 B.C.E.

Background

Babylon was Jehovah’s instrument before she became his target. Babylon was the “golden cup” by which Jehovah poured out his indignation against Judah and many nearby peoples, forcing them into servitude (Jer. 51:7 — jr, ch. 13, ¶9, pp. 157-158). Then Jehovah said, “I will repay Babylon … for all the evil that they have committed in Zion” (51:24).

Zedekiah was a vassal under oath. Nebuchadnezzar made him take an oath in Jehovah’s name to submit to the king of Babylon (2 Chron. 36:13; Ezek. 17:12, 13 — jr, ch. 2, ¶20, p. 26). He rebelled anyway, turning to Egypt (Ezek. 17:11-15, 17, 18). That broken oath is the centre of the week’s Treasures talk, “Learn From the Warning Example of Zedekiah” (jr 29).

The 70 years. Jehovah proclaimed through Jeremiah that the Jews would serve the Babylonians for 70 years, and then God would bring his people back to their land (Jer. 25:8-11; 29:10 — jr, ch. 13, ¶16, p. 162). Daniel used that prophecy to determine when “the devastations of Jerusalem” would end (Dan. 9:2), and Cyrus’ decree for the return came “in order that Jehovah’s word spoken by Jeremiah would be fulfilled” (Ezra 1:1).

Other details

  • Chapter 51:64 closes Jeremiah’s own words: “Down to this point are the words of Jeremiah.” Chapter 52 is a historical appendix.
  • Two names in chapter 51 are not identified in the research for this guide: Leb-kamai (51:1) and Sheshach (51:41). Do not state that they are code names for Chaldea or Babylon unless a source that says so is found and cited.
  • Ararat, Minni and Ashkenaz (51:27) are kingdoms summoned against Babylon; their locations were not verified from a library source in this research. Only their role in the verse and the direction “out of the north” (51:48) are verified.
  • Seraiah the quartermaster (51:59) is not Seraiah the chief priest (52:24), who was put to death at Riblah.
  • The names Bel and Merodach (Marduk) are Babylonian gods whose images are put to shame (50:2; 51:44, 47, 52).

Focus character — Who was Zedekiah?

Focus character

Zedekiah — born Mattaniah, son of Josiah by Hamutal, daughter of Jeremiah of Libnah (52:1). Nebuchadnezzar changed his name from Mattaniah to Zedekiah when he put him on the throne after Jehoiachin surrendered in 617 B.C.E. He was 21 years old when he became king and reigned 11 years in Jerusalem, 617–607 B.C.E. (52:1). He was the last earthly king of the Davidic line (jr, ch. 2, ¶18, pp. 25-26).

His family, as the Bible names them: his father Josiah; his mother Hamutal; his brothers Jehoahaz (Shallum) and Jehoiakim, who reigned before him; his nephew Jehoiachin, who reigned three months before him; and his sons, slaughtered before his eyes at Riblah (52:10). No family-tree diagram is included in this guide.

The routes, as the chapters describe them

  1. Jerusalem → the Arabah → the desert plains of Jericho → Riblah. Zedekiah and his soldiers fled by night through the gate between the double wall near the king’s garden, “by the way of the Arabah” (52:7); the Chaldean army overtook him in the desert plains of Jericho (52:8) and brought him up to Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah in the land of Hamath (52:9). Riblah lay on the north–south trade and military route between Egypt and the Euphrates (Insight, Vol. 2, “Riblah”), which is why both Pharaoh Nechoh and Nebuchadnezzar used it as a headquarters.
  2. Jerusalem → Babylon (exile). The exiles were taken to Babylon (52:15, 17, 27-30; 2 Chron. 36:20); Zedekiah was “brought … to Babylon, and kept … imprisoned until the day of his death” (52:11). The return journey is described at Ezra 1:1-3.
  3. Jerusalem → Babylon (Zedekiah and Seraiah, 614 B.C.E.). Zedekiah personally went to Babylon in the fourth year of his reign, accompanied by his quartermaster Seraiah (Insight, Vol. 2, “Zedekiah” and “Seraiah”); Jeremiah’s scroll against Babylon was read aloud there and thrown into the Euphrates (51:59-64).
  4. Babylon — the Euphrates through the city. The river ran through Babylon and fed its moat; Cyrus diverted it upstream and his army entered by the riverbed in 539 B.C.E. — the fulfilment of “I will dry up her sea” (51:36).
  5. The north → Babylon. The destroyers come “out of the north” (51:48); the Medes and Persians, with Ararat, Minni and Ashkenaz summoned (51:27-28), converge on Babylon from the north.

The places and routes above follow Insight on the Scriptures and the New World Translation text. No map image is included, and no route is drawn — because no map could be generated for this edition. Anything more precise than the descriptions above would go beyond the sources used here.

Focus location — Why did Riblah matter?

Riblah, in the land of Hamath, was where Nebuchadnezzar passed sentence on Zedekiah and where Judah’s leaders were executed. It mattered because it was Nebuchadnezzar’s military headquarters — the place from which he directed the siege of Jerusalem and to which prisoners were brought for judgment.

Insight on the Scriptures, Vol. 2, “Riblah”

“Riblah is that of the ruins near modern Ribleh on the E bank of the Orontes River, about 60 km (37 mi) NE of Baalbek, in the valley between the Lebanon and Anti-Lebanon mountains. … Riblah was a strategic location for a military camp. It dominated a N-S trade and military route between Egypt and the Euphrates.”

Nebuchadnezzar set up camp there after beginning the siege of Jerusalem in late 609 B.C.E.; Zedekiah was brought there in 607 B.C.E. Three things happened at Riblah in chapter 52:

  • Zedekiah was sentenced there (52:9): “Then they seized the king and brought him up to the king of Babylon at Riblah in the land of Hamath, and he passed sentence on him.”
  • His sons and the princes of Judah were slaughtered there before his eyes (52:10), and he was then blinded (52:11).
  • The priests and officials were put to death there (52:26-27): Seraiah the chief priest, Zephaniah the second priest, the three doorkeepers, a court official, seven close associates of the king, the army secretary and 60 men of the people were brought to the king of Babylon at Riblah, and “The king of Babylon struck them down and put them to death at Riblah in the land of Hamath. Thus Judah went into exile from its land.”

Riblah was therefore the place where the kingdom of Judah, as a political power, ended — its king blinded, its princes and priests killed, and its people sent into exile. Pharaoh Nechoh had used Riblah as a headquarters before Nebuchadnezzar, which underlines its standing as the strategic camp on the road between Egypt and the Euphrates.

How was Jeremiah involved?

Jeremiah was commissioned as prophet in the 13th year of King Josiah (647 B.C.E.) to warn the southern kingdom, Judah, of her impending destruction (Insight, Vol. 2, “Jeremiah, Book of,” p. 32). He prophesied in Jerusalem for 40 years, through Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin and Zedekiah, then in Egypt; his book was completed in 580 B.C.E. (All Scripture Is Inspired of God and Beneficial, Jeremiah introduction ¶2).

Jeremiah 1:2, 3

“The word of Jehovah came to him in the days of Josiah the son of Amon, the king of Judah, in the 13th year of his reign. It came also in the days of Jehoiakim the son of Josiah, the king of Judah, until the completion of the 11th year of Zedekiah the son of Josiah, the king of Judah, until Jerusalem went into exile in the fifth month.”

In these two chapters his involvement is direct and personal:

  • He wrote the words against Babylon in one book (51:60): “Jeremiah wrote in one book all the calamity that would come upon Babylon, all these words written against Babylon.”
  • He sent the book to Babylon by Seraiah (51:59-64), with instructions to read it aloud there, declare the place desolate forever, tie a stone to it and throw it into the Euphrates.
  • He was the prophet Zedekiah kept consulting — and imprisoning. During the final siege Zedekiah sought God’s guidance from Jeremiah, yet failed to act on it when told to surrender, and imprisoned him because he did not like the message (jr, p. 29, box). When the princes sought Jeremiah’s death, Zedekiah weakly acquiesced; Jeremiah was put into an empty water cistern, and Ebed-melech, an Ethiopian serving in the king’s house, rescued him (jr, ch. 2, ¶23, p. 28).
  • He recorded the fall itself. The last event Jeremiah recorded — the release of Jehoiachin — is dated 580 B.C.E. (Jer. 52:31-34 — jr, ch. 2, ¶27, p. 31). Insight, Vol. 2, “Jeremiah, Book of,” p. 32, notes that for the most part the book was not written at the time the prophecies were declared; Jeremiah was commanded in the fourth year of Jehoiakim (625 B.C.E.) to dictate all the words given him to date, and the remainder — including the proclamations uttered during Zedekiah’s reign and the accounts after Jerusalem’s fall, down to about 580 B.C.E. — was added later.
How did he cope?

The publications do not present Jeremiah as coping by softening the message or by withdrawing. They present him as holding to his commission and trusting the One who sent him.

  • He would not soften Jehovah’s message. God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 2, ¶23, p. 28: “… Jeremiah would still not soften Jehovah’s message. Therefore, the princes persuaded Zedekiah to put Jeremiah to death. They put the prophet into an empty water cistern where he could have perished in the deep mire. But Ebed-melech, an Ethiopian serving in the king’s house, rescued Jeremiah. (Jer. 38:4-13) How often Jehovah’s people in modern times have faced perils because of their conscientious refusal to get involved in political controversies! Clearly, Jeremiah’s experience can fortify you to face trials and overcome them.”
  • He trusted implicitly in the One who commissioned him. jr, ch. 2, ¶18, pp. 25-26: “… Clearly, Jeremiah had to trust implicitly in the One who had commissioned him as a prophet.”
  • He held to Jehovah’s promise. jr, ch. 2, ¶27, p. 31: “… Right from the beginning, Jehovah had told him: ‘They will be certain to fight against you, but they will not prevail against you, for “I am with you.”’ (Jer. 1:19) Our commission as Jehovah’s Witnesses today is like that of Jeremiah. So we can expect the reception to be similar. (Read Matthew 10:16-22.)”
  • He kept telling people what was about to happen. jr, ch. 13, ¶25, p. 167: “We certainly do not want to ‘steal away Jehovah’s words’ by concealing from people what is about to happen. (Read Jeremiah 23:30.) We are determined not to take away from his words their force and effect. Many prophecies that God had Jeremiah proclaim have already been fulfilled. This assures us that those remaining to be fulfilled are absolutely trustworthy.”

By the time he recorded the release of Jehoiachin, Jeremiah “must have been about 90 years old” (jr, ch. 2, ¶27, p. 31). The same paragraph adds: “… We have no reliable information concerning the end of his life. It is likely that he lived out his final years in Egypt and died faithful there after some 67 years in special service to Jehovah. … Did that make Jeremiah a failure? Far from it!”

Key accounts

Chapter 51 — A destructive wind against Babylon (51:1-4) — Jehovah raises up a destructive wind against Babylon and Leb-kamai; winnowers will empty her land; her young men are devoted to destruction.

“Flee out of the midst of Babylon” (51:5-10) — Israel and Judah are not widowed from their God (v. 5); flee and do not perish because of her error (v. 6); Babylon, the golden cup that made the nations drunk, has suddenly fallen (vv. 7-8); Jehovah has brought about justice for his people (v. 10).

The Medes stirred up; vengeance for the temple (51:11-14) — “Polish the arrows; take up the circular shields. Jehovah has stirred up the spirit of the kings of the Medes”; “this is the vengeance of Jehovah, the vengeance for his temple” (v. 11).

The Creator vs. the idols (51:15-19) — Jehovah, Maker of earth, sea and heavens (vv. 15-16); every metalworker put to shame by his carved image, “a falsehood … no spirit in them” (v. 17); idols are “a delusion, a work of mockery” and will perish (v. 18); “The Share of Jacob is not like these things” (v. 19).

Babylon as Jehovah’s war club — now herself to be repaid (51:20-26) — With “you” Jehovah smashed nations (vv. 20-23); then “I will repay Babylon … for all the evil that they have committed in Zion” (v. 24); the destructive mountain will become a burned-out mountain, desolate forever (vv. 25-26).

The nations summoned against her (51:27-33) — Ararat, Minni, Ashkenaz, the kings of Media (vv. 27-28); the land of Babylon to become “an object of horror, without an inhabitant” (v. 29); her warriors quit fighting, couriers report capture on every side (vv. 30-32); the daughter of Babylon is like a threshing floor, harvest time near (v. 33).

Zion’s complaint and Jehovah’s answer (51:34-37) — Nebuchadnezzar “has devoured me … swallowed me down like a big snake” (v. 34); Jehovah: “Here I am pleading your legal case, and I will execute vengeance for you. I will dry up her sea” (v. 36); Babylon will become “piles of stones, a lair of jackals” (v. 37).

The banquet and the lasting sleep (51:38-44) — Her princes made drunk, to sleep a lasting sleep (vv. 39, 57); Sheshach captured (v. 41); “I will turn my attention to Bel in Babylon, and I will take out of his mouth what he has swallowed” (v. 44).

“Get out of her midst, my people!” (51:45-50) — Escape from Jehovah’s burning anger (v. 45); do not be fainthearted about the reports, year after year (v. 46); heaven and earth shout joyfully over Babylon (v. 48); “You who escape the sword, keep going, do not stand still! Remember Jehovah from far away, And may Jerusalem come up into your heart” (v. 50).

The shame of the exiles, and the certainty of the fall (51:51-58) — “Even if Babylon should ascend to the heavens … From me her destroyers will come” (v. 53); “Jehovah is a God of retribution. He will surely repay” (v. 56); her broad wall “will be completely demolished, and her gates, though high, will be set on fire” (v. 58).

The scroll and the stone (51:59-64) — In the fourth year of Zedekiah, Jeremiah gave the written words against Babylon to Seraiah, who went with Zedekiah to Babylon; Seraiah was to read them aloud there, declare the place desolate forever, tie a stone to the book and throw it into the Euphrates: “This is how Babylon will sink down and never rise again.”

Chapter 52 — Zedekiah’s reign and rebellion (52:1-3) — 21 years old, reigned 11 years; did what was bad, as Jehoiakim had; rebelled against the king of Babylon.

The siege (52:4-6) — Ninth year, tenth month, tenth day: Nebuchadnezzar came with all his army, camped, built a siege wall; the city was under siege until the 11th year; fourth month, ninth day: severe famine, no food.

The flight and capture of Zedekiah (52:7-11) — Wall broken through; night flight by the Arabah; overtaken in the desert plains of Jericho; brought to Riblah; sons slaughtered before his eyes, princes slaughtered; blinded, bound with copper fetters, brought to Babylon, imprisoned until death. Parallel account: 2 Ki. 25:1-7.

The burning of Jerusalem (52:12-16) — Fifth month, tenth day, 19th year of Nebuchadnezzar: Nebuzaradan burned the house of Jehovah, the king’s house, all the houses; walls pulled down; exiles taken; the poorest left as vinedressers and compulsory labourers.

The temple vessels carried to Babylon (52:17-23) — Copper pillars, carriages, the copper Sea, the 12 copper bulls, and all gold and silver utensils taken; the copper “beyond weighing”; the pillars described in detail (18 cubits high, 12-cubit cord, four fingerbreadths thick, hollow; 96 pomegranates on the sides, 100 in all).

The officials executed at Riblah (52:24-27) — Seraiah the chief priest, Zephaniah the second priest, the three doorkeepers, a court official, seven close associates of the king, the army secretary, and 60 men of the people; struck down at Riblah. “Thus Judah went into exile from its land.”

The numbers of the exiles (52:28-30) — 7th year: 3,023 Jews; 18th year: 832 from Jerusalem; 23rd year: 745 Jews. In all, 4,600 people.

Jehoiachin released (52:31-34) — 37th year of Jehoiachin’s exile, 12th month, 25th day: Evil-merodach, in the year he became king, released Jehoiachin, spoke kindly with him, set his throne above the other captive kings, gave him new garments, a place at the king’s table and a daily allowance until his death. The book ends here.

Draw closer to Jehovah

Jehovah pleads his people’s case

Jeremiah 51:36

“Therefore this is what Jehovah says: ‘Here I am pleading your legal case, And I will execute vengeance for you. I will dry up her sea and make her wells dry.’”

Israel and Judah were “not widowed from their God” (51:5), even when their land was full of guilt. Jehovah is not a distant judge; he is the Repurchaser who pleads the case of his oppressed people (50:34) and gives rest to the land.

Jehovah is the Maker, not an idol

Jeremiah 51:15, 19

“He is the Maker of the earth by his power, The One who established the productive land by his wisdom And who stretched out the heavens by his understanding. … The Share of Jacob is not like these things, For he is the One who formed everything, Even the staff of his inheritance. Jehovah of armies is his name.”

The idols of Babylon are “a delusion, a work of mockery” with “no spirit in them” (51:17-18). The contrast draws us to the living God who made everything — and who can therefore undo what Babylon built.

Jehovah does what he has in mind — and shows kindness at the end

The book of Jeremiah does not end with the burning city. It ends with a king released from prison, spoken to kindly, seated above the other captive kings, eating before the king of Babylon and receiving a daily allowance until his death (52:31-34). Judgment is real, and so is Jehovah’s kindness to those who belong to him.

Zedekiah was “dominated by his princes and his own fears” (jr 29). He consulted Jeremiah in secret so as not to upset his princes, and weakly acquiesced when they sought Jeremiah’s death. Do not let fear of what people will say, or of mistreatment, keep you from obeying God — Zedekiah himself admitted he feared that if he obeyed God, the people would mistreat him (Jer. 37:15-17; 38:4, 5, 14-19, 24-26).
