Jeremiah 40 & 41 — A Remnant, a Governor, and a Murder at Mizpah

Study note · compiled Oct 03, 2026 · structure follows God’s Word Is Truth—A Handbook to the Bible · Bible reading for the week of Oct 05-11, 2026 · Treasures theme: "Gaining a Proper Perspective of Jehovah's Protection"

"Jehovah your God foretold this calamity against this place, and Jehovah has brought it about just as he said."
Key text — Jeremiah 40:2, 3 (spoken by Nebuzaradan, the Babylonian chief of the guard)
Focus

Jerusalem lies in ashes. Jeremiah is freed at Ramah and chooses to stay with the poor remnant under Governor Gedaliah at Mizpah; a brief recovery ends when Ishmael murders the governor and the survivors turn toward Egypt.

Connection to the Bible’s theme

Jehovah’s word proves true even in the mouth of a Babylonian officer, and he protects his servants in the way his purpose requires — preserving his prophet while allowing a God-fearing governor to die.

Timeline of events

609 B.C.E. — Zedekiah's 9th year, 10th month: Babylon's final siege of Jerusalem begins (39:1).
607 B.C.E., 4th month — On the 9th day the city wall is breached; Zedekiah is captured, blinded and taken to Babylon (39:2-7).
607 B.C.E., 5th month — The temple and city are burned, the walls torn down, and most of the people deported (2 Ki. 25:8-11; Jer. 39:8, 9).
607 B.C.E. — Chapter 40:1-6 — Jeremiah, in handcuffs among the exiles at Ramah, is freed by Nebuzaradan, given food and a gift, and goes to Gedaliah at Mizpah.
607 B.C.E. — Chapter 40:7-12 — the army chiefs and scattered Jews gather to Gedaliah; the remnant gathers a very great harvest.
607 B.C.E. — Chapter 40:13-16 — Johanan warns Gedaliah of Ishmael's plot; Gedaliah refuses to believe it.
607 B.C.E., 7th month — Chapter 41 — Ishmael murders Gedaliah at a meal, kills 80 pilgrims, and carries off the people of Mizpah; Johanan rescues them at Gibeon; the group camps near Bethlehem, heading for Egypt.
c. Oct 01, 607 B.C.E. — With the remnant gone, Judah is left completely desolate; the 70 years of desolation begin.
537 B.C.E., 7th month — The first exiles are back in Judah, 70 years from the start of the full desolation (Ezra 3:1).

1.Why Valuable

These two chapters are short and record no words of Jeremiah, yet they show Jehovah’s word fulfilled to the letter and teach a balanced view of how he protects his people. They also open the final movement of the book: everything in chapters 42-44 flows from the murder at Mizpah. Above all, the account reveals Jehovah’s qualities:

  • His word is true. Even a Babylonian officer had to admit it: "Jehovah your God foretold this calamity against this place, and Jehovah has brought it about just as he said, because you people sinned against Jehovah and did not obey his voice" (40:2, 3). Jehovah's judgments are so plainly fulfilled that outsiders recognize them.
  • He keeps his promises to his loyal ones. He had told Jeremiah, "'I am with you to save you,' declares Jehovah" (1:8). Freed from handcuffs and given food and a gift (40:4, 5), Jeremiah experienced that promise in full. "What is the point? Jehovah promised protection and fulfilled his promise." (jr 189 ¶16)
  • He is generous, even after discipline. The remnant "gathered wine and summer fruits in very great quantity" (40:12). In the very year of the destruction, the land still yielded richly — evidence that he had given his people "a good land" (Deut. 8:7, 8).
  • He is merciful. He allowed a remnant to stay, and dispersed Jews "began returning from all the places to which they had been dispersed" (40:12). Even after judgment, he opened a way for the survivors to live in peace in the land.
  • He is wise in how he protects. He preserved Jeremiah's life, yet he did not prevent the death of Gedaliah, a God-fearing man (41:1, 2). "The promise of divine protection does not mean that Jehovah is obligated to work miracles in our behalf. No, our God does not guarantee us a problem-free life in this old system." (cl 72-73 ¶13) He has reasons that reach beyond the moment: "If Jehovah were to use his power to effect miraculous deliverance in all cases, Satan would no doubt taunt Jehovah and call into question the genuineness of our devotion to our God." (cl 72-73 ¶13)
  • He deals with each servant individually. "Clearly, we cannot expect our God to protect all his servants in identical ways." (cl 72-73 ¶14) Jeremiah, Ebed-melech and Gedaliah each experienced Jehovah's care differently.

2.What to Know Before Reading

When. Both chapters fall in 607 B.C.E., in the months right after Jerusalem's destruction. Chapter 40 opens after Nebuzaradan "let him go free from Ramah" (40:1) — Ramah being where, it appears, the captives were assembled before the march to Babylon. Chapter 41 is dated "in the seventh month" (41:1), the month of Ethanim or Tishri (September-October), only about two months after the temple was burned. The killing of Gedaliah and the flight that followed left the land fully desolate, which marks the start of the 70 years of desolation.

On the dating: Insight explains that the people left in the land "did so until the assassination of Gedaliah, Nebuchadnezzar's appointee, whereupon they fled into Egypt, finally leaving Judah completely desolate. (2Ki 25:9-12, 22-26) This was in the seventh month, Ethanim (or Tishri, corresponding to parts of September and October). Hence the count of the 70 years of desolation must have begun about October 1, 607 B.C.E., ending in 537 B.C.E." (it "Chronology")

Who was Gedaliah?

A third-generation reformer. Gedaliah is named three times over as “the son of Ahikam the son of Shaphan” (40:5, 9, 11; 41:2) — a pedigree, not a formality. His grandfather Shaphan was the royal secretary who received “the book of the Law” from Hilkiah and read it before young King Josiah, setting off the last great reform of Judah (2 Ki. 22:8-13). Insight traces the line forward: “Shaphan’s sons Ahikam (Jer 26:24), Elasah (Jer 29:1-3), and Gemariah (Jer 36:10-12, 25) apparently were also adherents to true worship. His son Jaazaniah was not, however. (Eze 8:10, 11) Shaphan’s grandson Gedaliah was the God-fearing governor appointed after Jerusalem’s fall.” (it "Shaphan")

A family that stood by Jeremiah. When priests and prophets demanded Jeremiah’s death for prophesying against the temple, “Ahikam the son of Shaphan supported Jeremiah, so that he was not handed over to the people to be put to death” (26:24). Gemariah lent his temple chamber for Baruch’s public reading of the scroll and pleaded with Jehoiakim not to burn it (36:10, 25); Elasah carried Jeremiah’s letter to the exiles in Babylon (29:3). The prophet released at Ramah was walking to the house of a family that had protected him for twenty years — which is why Nebuchadnezzar had already “handed him over to Gedaliah the son of Ahikam the son of Shaphan to be brought to his house” (39:14).

The governor. “King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon appointed Gedaliah the son of Ahikam the son of Shaphan over the people whom he had left behind in the land of Judah” (2 Ki. 25:22). Insight sums up his brief rule: “Gedaliah established his residence at Mizpah, and here the prophet Jeremiah took up dwelling. … Gedaliah assured them, under oath, that it would go well with them as long as they continued serving the king of Babylon, and he encouraged them to gather wine, oil, and summer fruits. Even the Jews who were dispersed in Moab, Ammon, Edom, and other places continued coming to Gedaliah.” (it "Gedaliah" No. 4) His oath (40:9) echoed exactly what Jeremiah had preached for years: submit to Babylon and live (27:11, 12).

His one failing. Warned twice, he answered Johanan: “what you are saying concerning Ishmael is a lie” (40:16). “So when Ishmael, along with ten other men, came to Mizpah, Gedaliah took no precautions. He proceeded to eat with them; and while they were eating, Ishmael and the men with him rose up and killed Gedaliah as well as all the Jews and Chaldeans who were with Gedaliah.” (it "Gedaliah" No. 4) A good man’s trust, not his wickedness, cost the remnant its last chance in the land.

Remembered for centuries. His death was mourned by a fast kept into Zechariah’s day: “The fast of the seventh month” was apparently held as a sad remembrance of Gedaliah’s death or of the complete desolation of the land following Gedaliah’s assassination when the remaining Jews, out of fear of the Babylonians, went down into Egypt. (it "Fast") Jehovah promised that this fast, with the others marking Jerusalem’s fall, would one day become “occasions for exultation and joy” (Zech. 7:5; 8:19).

Writer: Jeremiah himself — a prisoner at the start of chapter 40, a free man by its sixth verse.

What he was doing. At the start of chapter 40 he was a prisoner in handcuffs, swept up with the exiles at Ramah (40:1). Once freed, he made a choice: rather than take Nebuzaradan's offer of care in Babylon, he went to Gedaliah at Mizpah "and stayed with him among the people who were left in the land" (40:4-6). jr sums it up: "Later, Jeremiah got mixed in with some Jews being taken off into captivity, but he was released. There was yet more for him to do in God's service; he still had work to do among the survivors." (jr chap. 2 ¶25)

How he coped.

  • He relied on Jehovah's promise. "Jeremiah had not forgotten God's assurance: 'I am with you to deliver you.' (Jer. 1:8) With Almighty God guarding those two loyal men, neither human enemies nor hunger would finish them off." (jr 189 ¶16) Even in handcuffs among the deportees, that promise held.
  • He chose the place of need over the place of comfort. Babylon offered security and care (40:4); Mizpah offered poverty and uncertainty. He stayed with the poor of the land (40:6), where his service was still needed.
  • He accepted help graciously. He received "a food allowance and a gift" from a Babylonian official (40:5), recognizing that Jehovah could provide even through unexpected people.
  • He kept going after national disaster. Everything he had warned of had happened; his city and temple were gone. He did not retire from his assignment but stayed with the people who still needed Jehovah's word — as chapter 42 will show, they soon came to him for direction.

Inference: Chapters 40-41 record no words of Jeremiah. Since he lived at Mizpah, he was likely among "all the people left in Mizpah" whom Ishmael took captive and Johanan rescued (41:10, 14, 16), which would explain why he was with the group that later asked him to pray for them (42:1, 2).

Background: Babylon left “some of the poorest people” in Judah and gave them vineyards and fields to work (39:10). Gedaliah, from a family that had stood by Jeremiah before (26:24), governed from Mizpah under Babylonian appointment, and Jews who had fled to Moab, Ammon and Edom began to return. The parallel account is 2 Kings 25:22-26.

Other details: Chapters 40-44 form one unit, the aftermath of the fall; chapter 41 is the only part of it dated (“in the seventh month,” 41:1). Within this guide, citations without a book name are to Jeremiah.

3.Summary

Jeremiah is freed and goes to Mizpah. (40:1-6) In 607 B.C.E., Nebuzaradan finds Jeremiah in handcuffs among the exiles at Ramah, credits Jehovah for the calamity, and releases him with a food allowance and a gift. Offered care in Babylon, Jeremiah instead goes to Gedaliah at Mizpah “and stayed with him among the people who were left in the land” (40:6).

The remnant gathers and the land yields. (40:7-12) Gedaliah swears to the army chiefs, “Do not be afraid of serving the Chaldeans” (40:9). Jews scattered in Moab, Ammon and Edom come home, and the remnant gathers “wine and summer fruits in very great quantity” (40:12).

A warning dismissed. (40:13-16) Johanan reports that Baalis of Ammon has sent Ishmael to kill the governor and offers to strike first. Gedaliah refuses: “what you are saying concerning Ishmael is a lie” (40:16).

Murder at Mizpah and the flight toward Egypt. (41:1-18) In the seventh month Ishmael and ten men kill Gedaliah at a meal, with the Jews and Chaldean soldiers present (41:1-3). Two days later he slaughters 80 pilgrims and fills King Asa’s cistern with the bodies (41:4-9), then marches the people of Mizpah toward Ammon. Johanan overtakes him “by the great waters in Gibeon” (41:12); the captives are freed, Ishmael escapes, and the survivors, afraid of Babylon’s reprisal, camp near Bethlehem “intending to go on into Egypt” (41:17, 18).

4.Benefit From Jeremiah 40-41

1. Draw closer to Jehovah

  1. A Babylonian officer said, “Jehovah has brought it about just as he said” (40:2, 3). How does it affect your trust in Jehovah to know that he always keeps his word, even when he does not remove every trial? (40:2-4; jr 189 ¶16)
  2. Jehovah preserved Jeremiah (40:4, 5) but did not prevent Gedaliah’s death (41:2). What does this teach you about how Jehovah protects his servants today? (41:1, 2; it "Gedaliah" No. 4; cl 72-73 ¶13-14; Prov. 4:5, 6; 1 Thess. 5:14)
  3. In the very year of judgment the land still yielded richly (40:12). What does that tell you about Jehovah’s generosity? (Deut. 8:7, 8; w06 6/15 16 ¶4)

2. Deepen your understanding

The question: Jer 40:12 — How does this verse confirm that Jehovah gave his people the good land described in Deuteronomy 8:6-8?

Moses had promised Israel "a good land… a land of wheat and barley, of grapevines, fig trees, and pomegranates, a land of olive oil and honey" (Deut. 8:7, 8). Nearly nine centuries later, that promise was still visible. The Watchtower cites a reference work: "Vines were so abundant in ancient Palestine that grape seeds have been found in most, if not all, of the excavated sites." It continues: "The vines of the Promised Land proved so productive that even during the year 607 B.C.E. when Nebuchadnezzar's armies devastated Judah, the people remaining in the land 'went gathering wine and summer fruits in very great quantity.'" (w06 6/15 16 ¶4)

The point: in the very year when the nation was judged, the land itself still showed how generously Jehovah had provided. The ruin came from the people's unfaithfulness, not from any failure in what he had given.

Ramah and "Rachel weeping." Jeremiah was freed at Ramah (40:1), where "it appears that captive Jews assembled… after its devastation in 607 B.C.E. Some prisoners may even have been executed at Ramah. That may have occasioned an initial fulfillment" of Jeremiah 31:15, "as if it were Rachel weeping over the loss of her 'sons.'" Matthew later applied the same words to Herod's massacre of the infants of Bethlehem (Matt. 2:16-18). (jr chap. 13 ¶17)

Treasures outline for the week: Jehovah preserved Jeremiah's life (Jer 40:2-4; jr 189 ¶16) · Jehovah did not preserve Gedaliah's life, although he was a God-fearing man (Jer 41:1, 2; it "Gedaliah" No. 4) · Jehovah does not always miraculously deliver us from trials, sickness, or death (cl 72-73 ¶13-14). For meditation: How does Jehovah protect us as individuals today? (Prov 4:5, 6; 1 Thess 5:14). Bible reading: Jer 40:1-10.

3. Apply in your life

Imitate Jehovah’s qualities.

  • Keep your word. Jehovah's promise to Jeremiah held through siege, famine and chains (1:8; 40:4). When we keep our promises to family, friends and the congregation, we reflect his reliability.
  • Be generous to those who have lost much. Jeremiah was given "a food allowance and a gift" (40:5), and the remnant were given land and a harvest (39:10; 40:12). We imitate Jehovah's generosity when we give practical help to brothers and sisters after disasters, illness or loss.
  • Welcome those who are returning. Jehovah let the scattered Jews come home (40:11, 12). We can show the same mercy by warmly welcoming those who return to the congregation after a time away.
  • Support others realistically in their trials. Jehovah does not promise a problem-free life (cl 72-73 ¶13), but he always sustains his servants. We can imitate him by not making empty promises to someone who is suffering, but by staying close, speaking consolingly and supporting them, as Paul urged: "Speak consolingly to those who are depressed, support the weak, be patient toward all" (1 Thess. 5:14).
  • Treat each person as an individual. Jehovah protected each of his servants in a way suited to them (cl 72-73 ¶14). In shepherding, teaching and encouraging others, we can take time to understand each person's situation rather than offering one-size-fits-all help.

Other lessons.

  • Gain a proper perspective of Jehovah's protection. This is the week's Treasures theme. Jehovah preserved Jeremiah (40:2-4) but did not preserve Gedaliah (41:1, 2), and "Jehovah does not always miraculously deliver us from trials, sickness, or death." His protection is above all spiritual, safeguarding our relationship with him: "Do not forsake it, and it will protect you. Love it, and it will safeguard you" (Prov. 4:6).
  • Take warnings seriously. Gedaliah heard a clear warning twice and dismissed it as "a lie" (40:14-16). Insight notes that when Ishmael came, "Gedaliah took no precautions." (it "Gedaliah" No. 4) "The shrewd one sees the danger and conceals himself" (Prov. 22:3). Kindness and trust are good, but they should not make us brush aside well-founded counsel.
  • Do not judge by outward show. Ishmael went out "weeping as he walked along" to greet the pilgrims, then slaughtered them (41:6, 7). Outward displays of feeling do not always reveal what is in the heart.
  • Serve where the need is. Jeremiah chose to stay with the poor of the land rather than take an easier life in Babylon (40:4-6). Our own choices about where and how we serve can reflect the same spirit.
  • Watch how fear shapes decisions. After the murder, the survivors "had become afraid" of the Chaldeans and headed toward Egypt (41:17, 18) — a decision made before they asked Jehovah. Chapters 42-44 show where that path led.
  • Draw comfort from the Bible's accuracy. An Ammonite seal impression bearing the name "Baalyisha" has been found; Insight says that "Baalyisha is possibly the Baalis of the Bible." (it "Baalis") Details like this support the historical reliability of the account.
