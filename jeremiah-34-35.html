Jeremiah 34 & 35 — Broken Promises and the Faithful Rechabites

Study note · compiled Sep 22, 2026 · Bible reading for the week of Sep 14-20, 2026 · Treasures theme: "Jehovah Rewards Faithful Obedience"

"There will never fail to be a descendant of Jehonadab the son of Rechab to serve in my presence."
Key text — Jeremiah 35:19

Focus: Two chapters, two kinds of obedience, set side by side. In chapter 34 the people of Jerusalem, with Babylon's army at the walls, suddenly keep the Law and free their Hebrew slaves — then take them back the moment the danger lifts. In chapter 35 a family of tent-dwellers, the Rechabites, refuse a cup of wine offered to them in the temple itself, because a forefather who lived more than two centuries earlier told them not to drink it. Jehovah uses the one to expose the other, and he rewards the family that obeyed. The note follows five study questions: the setting; what Jeremiah was doing and how he coped; what the account tells us about Jehovah; how we can imitate Jehovah's qualities; and other ways the account benefits us.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When. The two chapters are not in time order. Chapter 34 belongs to the final siege under King Zedekiah, when Babylon's armies "were fighting against Jerusalem and against all the cities of Judah that were left, against Lachish and against Azekah" (34:7) — about 609-608 B.C.E. Chapter 35 steps back to "the days of Jehoiakim" (35:1), who reigned 628-618 B.C.E., when Nebuchadnezzar had come up against the land and the Rechabites had moved into Jerusalem for safety (35:11). Placed next to each other, the chapters make a sharp contrast.

What was going on. With the city surrounded, Zedekiah and his princes tried to win God's favor by honoring a Law they had long ignored: a Hebrew slave was to go free after six years of service (34:14; Ex. 21:2). They sealed the covenant in the temple by the solemn rite of cutting a calf in two and passing between the halves (34:15, 18, 19). Then an Egyptian army marched out, the Babylonians pulled back, and the owners took their slaves back (34:11; 37:5). Jehovah's answer was pointed: since they had withdrawn "liberty" from their brothers, he would "proclaim liberty" to them — "to the sword, to pestilence, and to famine" (34:17). Chapter 35 supplies the contrast: a family who had kept a far harder rule for generations, simply because their forefather asked it of them.

2.What was Jeremiah doing — and how did he cope?

What he was doing. He carried a hard message to the king in the middle of a war — Jerusalem would be burned and Zedekiah would "see the king of Babylon eye to eye" (34:2, 3, 6). He denounced the broken covenant in front of princes, priests and people (34:12-22). Earlier, under Jehoiakim, he had carried out an unusual assignment: bringing a whole family into a temple dining room and setting "cups and goblets full of wine" before them (35:2-5).

How he coped.

  • He obeyed his own instructions promptly. Told to go to the Rechabites, he records simply, "So I took Jaazaniah…" (35:3). The prophet who spoke about obedience practised it.
  • He delivered the message as given, to the powerful as well as the ordinary. The princes, court officials and priests are named in the judgment (34:19); he did not soften it for rank.
  • He let Jehovah's object lesson do the teaching. Rather than argue, he set the Rechabites' faithfulness in front of the nation (35:12-17), an example that anyone could understand.
  • He passed on good news as faithfully as bad. The same prophet who announced calamity also carried Jehovah's warm promise to the Rechabite household (35:18, 19).

3.What does this account tell me about Jehovah?

  • He notices what is right. Even in a disobedient nation, he saw the good step and said so: "Recently you yourselves turned around and did what was right in my eyes" (34:15). He does not overlook sincere efforts to do what pleases him.
  • He looks at the heart, not the show. He saw through obedience that lasted only as long as the danger: "when they were in danger, the Jews seemed to observe divine statutes, as if that could offset their former conduct. But when the danger subsided, they went back to their old ways." (jr 49-50 ¶14)
  • He takes promises made in his name seriously. The covenant was made "before me in the house that bears my name," and breaking it "profaned my name" (34:15, 16). His name, and what is attached to it, matters to him.
  • He is just. His judgment fit the sin exactly: "You have not obeyed me in proclaiming liberty… So I will now proclaim liberty to you" (34:17). The Babylonians who had withdrawn would come back, "fight against it and capture it and burn it with fire" (34:21, 22).
  • He is patient and persistent. "I have spoken to you again and again… And I kept sending all my servants the prophets to you, sending them again and again, saying, 'Turn back, please'" (35:14, 15). He kept reaching out long after most would have stopped.
  • He is a patient Teacher. Rather than only condemn, he gave Judah a lesson anyone could understand, a family that kept its word: "Were you not continually urged to obey my words?" (35:13-16).
  • He appreciates loyalty and rewards it. "Jehovah was pleased with the respectful obedience they showed. Their unswerving obedience to an earthly father stood in contrast to the disobedience of the Judeans to their Creator." (it "Rechabites" ¶5) His promise: "There will never fail to be a descendant of Jehonadab the son of Rechab to serve in my presence" (35:19).

For meditation: How does it make me feel that Jehovah notices even small steps in the right direction, and that he rewards faithfulness that no one else may see?

4.How can I imitate Jehovah's qualities?

  • Notice and commend what others do well. Jehovah said, "You… did what was right in my eyes" (34:15), and he openly praised the Rechabites (35:18). Specific, sincere commendation of a brother, a child or a Bible student builds them up.
  • Keep your word, as Jehovah keeps his. He remembered the covenant he made with their forefathers "in the day I brought them out of the land of Egypt" (34:13), and he kept his promise to the Rechabites (35:19). A dedication vow, marriage vows and ordinary promises all deserve that seriousness.
  • Be fair to those under your authority. Jehovah required liberty "each one to his brother and to his fellow man" (34:17), and he held the powerful to account for exploiting the weak. Employers, parents and anyone with authority can imitate his justice by never taking advantage of those who depend on them.
  • Be patient and persistent with people. Jehovah spoke "again and again" (35:14, 15). In the ministry, with family members or with a slow-progressing Bible student, we can keep reaching out rather than give up after a few refusals.
  • Teach with clear examples. Jehovah used the Rechabites to make his point unmistakable (35:12-16). When we teach, a well-chosen example often reaches the heart better than argument.
  • Value sincerity in yourself. Jehovah wants obedience from the heart, not only in a crisis. We imitate him by examining our own motives and serving him in easy times as fully as in hard ones.

5.What other ways can this account benefit me?

  • Obey Jehovah consistently, not only when trouble presses. Judah's covenant lasted as long as the danger did (34:10, 11). The workbook's question is searching: "Am I resolved to obey Jehovah, even if at times I feel I could avoid the consequences of disobedience?"
  • Let obedience come from the heart. Of those slave owners, jr observes: "Despite their pretense of accepting the spirit of the Law, their later actions revealed that at heart they did not want to comply with direction found in God's Word and be molded by it." (jr 49-50 ¶14)
  • Obey when no one is checking. Jehonadab could not see whether his descendants drank. "To the Rechabites, obeying their long-dead ancestor was important. With even greater diligence, true worshippers should obey the commands of the living God." (jr 76 ¶18)
  • Obedience is protection. "Is it not reasonable that those who strictly obey Jehovah are assured of his protection during the great tribulation?" (jr 76 ¶18; 35:19)
  • A family head's example can last for generations. Jehonadab's stand for pure worship in Jehu's day was still shaping his family more than two centuries later (35:6-10, 16).
  • The Bible's history can be trusted. The Lachish Letters confirm the very situation described at 34:7 (see the Closer look below).

Closer look — Lachish and Azekah (Spiritual Gems)

The question: Jer 34:7 — What archaeological evidence supports the events described in this verse?

Jeremiah 34:7 names the last two fortified cities still holding out besides Jerusalem: Lachish and Azekah. Excavation has illustrated that very moment. "Excavations at Lachish produced evidence of destruction by fire twice within a period of a few years, believed to represent two attacks by the Babylonians." In the ashes of the second burning were found 21 inscribed potsherds, the Lachish Letters. Letter IV reads: "We are watching for the signals of Lachish, according to all the indications which my lord hath given, for we cannot see Azekah." Insight comments: "This remarkably expresses the situation described at Jeremiah 34:7, quoted earlier, and indicates that Azekah had already fallen or at least was failing to send out the fire or smoke signals expected." (it "Archaeology" ¶27-28)

Key passages (New World Translation)

Jeremiah 34:8-11

“The word that came to Jeremiah from Jehovah after King Zedekiah had made a covenant with all the people in Jerusalem to proclaim liberty to them, that everyone should free his Hebrew slaves, male and female, so that no one would keep a fellow Jew as his slave. So all the princes and all the people obeyed. They had entered into the covenant that everyone should free his male and female slaves and not keep them as slaves any longer. They obeyed and let them go. However, they later brought back the male and female slaves whom they had freed, and they again forced them back into slavery.”

Jeremiah 34:15-17

“And recently you yourselves turned around and did what was right in my eyes by proclaiming liberty to your fellow men, and you made a covenant before me in the house that bears my name. But then you turned around and profaned my name by bringing back your male and female slaves whom you had freed according to their desire, and you forced them back into slavery.’ “Therefore this is what Jehovah says: ‘You have not obeyed me in proclaiming liberty, each one to his brother and to his fellow man. So I will now proclaim liberty to you,’ declares Jehovah, ‘to the sword, to pestilence, and to famine, and I will make you an object of horror to all the kingdoms of the earth.”

Jeremiah 35:5-8

“Then I put cups and goblets full of wine before the men of the house of the Rechabites and said to them: “Drink wine.” But they said: “We will not drink wine, because Jehonadab the son of Rechab, our forefather, gave us this command, ‘Neither you nor your sons must ever drink wine. And you must not build a house, sow seed, or plant or obtain a vineyard. Instead, you must always dwell in tents, so that you may live for a long time in the land where you are residing as foreigners.’ So we continue to obey the voice of Jehonadab the son of our forefather Rechab in all that he commanded us, by never drinking any wine—we, our wives, our sons, and our daughters.”

Jeremiah 35:14

“Jehonadab the son of Rechab commanded his descendants not to drink wine, and they have carried out his words by not drinking it to this day, thus obeying the order of their forefather. However, I have spoken to you again and again, but you have not obeyed me.

Jeremiah 35:18, 19

“And Jeremiah said to the household of the Rechabites: “This is what Jehovah of armies, the God of Israel, says, ‘Because you have obeyed the order of your forefather Jehonadab and you continue to observe all his orders, doing exactly what he ordered you, this is what Jehovah of armies, the God of Israel, says: “There will never fail to be a descendant of Jehonadab the son of Rechab to serve in my presence.”’””
