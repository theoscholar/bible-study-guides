Jeremiah 42-44 — They Asked, but They Did Not Listen

Study note · compiled Oct 05, 2026 · structure follows God’s Word Is Truth—A Handbook to the Bible · Bible reading for the week of Oct 12-18, 2026 · Treasures theme: "They Asked, but They Did Not Listen"

“Whether good or bad, we will obey the voice of Jehovah our God, to whom we are sending you, so that it may go well with us because we obey the voice of Jehovah our God.”
Key text — Jeremiah 42:6 (the remnant’s vow, ten days before they broke it)
Focus

The survivors of Judah, terrified after Gedaliah’s murder, ask Jeremiah to seek Jehovah’s direction and swear to obey it. Ten days later the answer comes — stay in the land and live — and they call it a lie, march everyone down to Egypt, and there defend their worship of the Queen of Heaven. Jehovah answers with a sign: Pharaoh Hophra himself will fall.

Connection to the Bible’s theme

Jehovah’s word, not man’s, is what comes true (44:28). The remnant’s choice between Judah and Egypt is the choice every generation faces between Jehovah’s direction and its own fears — and the chapters show that his sovereignty reaches into Egypt as surely as into Jerusalem.

1.Why Valuable

These three chapters close the story of the land: the last Judeans walk out of it with the prophet in tow, and Jehovah’s word follows them to the Nile. They are the Bible’s clearest study of what it means to ask for direction and then refuse it — the Treasures theme for the week. Above all, they reveal Jehovah’s qualities:

  • He answers those who ask — in his time. “Now ten days later the word of Jehovah came to Jeremiah” (42:7). The remnant wanted an answer at once; Jehovah gave one that was complete, and gave it when it would test whether their vow was sincere.
  • He is ready to heal what he has had to wound. “If you will indeed remain in this land, then I will build you up and not tear you down, and I will plant you and not uproot you, for I will feel regret over the calamity I have caused you” (42:10). The very words of Jeremiah’s commission (1:10) are turned, forty years on, into an offer of mercy.
  • He is a Protector who asks for trust. “Do not be afraid because of him, … for I am with you, to save you and to rescue you out of his hand” (42:11). The fear that drove the remnant was real; Jehovah did not deny it — he answered it with himself.
  • He is patient to the point of pleading. “I kept sending all my servants the prophets to you, sending them again and again, saying: ‘Please do not do this detestable thing that I hate’” (44:4). Please — from the Sovereign of the universe, to idolaters.
  • He is Sovereign over Egypt as over Judah. Nebuchadnezzar is “my servant,” and Jehovah will “set the houses of the gods of Egypt on fire” (43:10, 12). The refuge the remnant chose was inside his jurisdiction all along.
  • His word is the one that comes true. “Then all the remnant of Judah who came to the land of Egypt to reside there will know whose word has come true, mine or theirs!” (44:28). He even gives them a sign to watch for — the fall of Pharaoh Hophra (44:29, 30).

2.What to Know Before Reading

When. Chapters 42 and 43 follow directly on 41: the remnant is still camped at Geruth Chimham near Bethlehem in the autumn of 607 B.C.E., “in the seventh month” of Gedaliah’s murder (41:1, 17). Jehovah’s answer comes “ten days later” (42:7), and the march to Tahpanhes and the sign at Pharaoh’s house belong to the same season. Chapter 44 is undated; it is addressed to Jews already settled across Lower and Upper Egypt (44:1, 15), so some time had passed. Insight: “After the Jews fled to Egypt in 607 B.C.E., Jehovah said by the mouth of Jeremiah: ‘Here I am giving Pharaoh Hophra, the king of Egypt, into the hand of his enemies and into the hand of those seeking for his soul.’ (Jer 44:1, 26, 30) This was to be a sign of imminent calamity to come over the Jews dwelling in Egypt.” (it "Hophra")

Who was Johanan?

The rescuer who would not be ruled. Johanan son of Kareah is the human centre of chapters 40-44. Insight gathers his record: “One of the chiefs of the military forces remaining in Judah after the general deportation to Babylon in the summer of 607 B.C.E. This son of Kareah readily supported the appointment of Gedaliah and, on learning of Ishmael’s plot to assassinate the governor, asked Gedaliah for permission to kill Ishmael secretly but was denied it. (Jer 40:7, 8, 13-16) Gedaliah was assassinated, Johanan led the forces to avenge him, and persons whom Ishmael had taken captive were recovered; but the assassin himself escaped to Ammon. (Jer 41:11-16) Fearing reprisals from the Babylonians, Johanan and the others asked the prophet Jeremiah what they should do, but, rather than follow Jehovah’s advice to remain in the land, they fled to Egypt, taking Jeremiah with them.​—Jer 42:1–43:7; 2Ki 25:23-26.” (it "Johanan" No. 5)

Right about Ishmael, wrong about Jehovah. Twice Johanan read a situation correctly where Gedaliah did not (40:13-16), and his pursuit to Gibeon saved every captive (41:11-14). That record makes his next step the harder to watch: he had already “intended to go on into Egypt” before he asked (41:17), and when the answer contradicted the plan he called the prophet a liar and Baruch an agitator (43:2, 3). The man who could not persuade a governor to take a threat seriously could not himself take Jehovah’s word seriously once it crossed his fear. His companion Azariah is the same man the account calls Jezaniah (it "Azariah" No. 20): “Azariah was one of the chiefs of the military forces who supported Gedaliah (Jer 40:7-10); one who requested Jeremiah to pray in their behalf for direction (Jer 42:1-3); and, finally, one of ‘the presumptuous men’ who repudiated Jehovah’s answer by the mouth of Jeremiah.​—Jer 43:1-3.”

What became of him. The Bible does not record Johanan’s death. Jehovah’s word was that the men “determined to go to Egypt to reside there will die by the sword, by famine, and by pestilence” (42:17), and Insight notes the end of the story: “the fulfillment of Jehovah’s prophecies caught up with the Israelite refugees when Nebuchadnezzar marched against Egypt and conquered the land.​—Jer 43:8-13; 46:13-26.” (it "Egypt, Egyptian")

Johanan’s age, family and tribe are not given; he appears only in 2 Kings 25:23 and Jeremiah 40-43.

Why Egypt? — and why Jehovah said no

What Egypt promised. The remnant said it plainly: in Egypt “we will not see war or hear the sound of the horn or hunger for bread; there is where we will live” (42:14). Egypt was the one great power Babylon had not yet subdued, it lay beyond Nebuchadnezzar’s reach as they supposed, and Jews were already living there (it "Egypt, Egyptian": “the remnant of Judah’s population fled to Egypt as a sanctuary, evidently joining Jews already in that land. (Jer 24:1, 8-10)”). Tahpanhes, where they stopped first, was “apparently a fortress city in the Delta region” (it "Egypt, Egyptian"), which Insight identifies with the Greek Daphnae, “an important fortified city on Egypt’s eastern border” (it "Tahpanes").

Why it was the one place they must not go. For forty years Jeremiah had said that Egypt was a broken reed. “During the last years of the Judean kingdom, the prophet Jeremiah consistently warned his people against political alliances with Egypt or reliance on Egypt for help against the rising power of Babylon.” (it "Tahpanes") The remnant’s own eyes had seen it: Pharaoh’s army had marched out to relieve the siege and gone home again (37:5-10). To run to Egypt now was to repeat the sin that had cost Jerusalem the temple — trusting the arm of flesh over the word of God — and, as chapter 44 shows, to carry Jerusalem’s idolatry with them.

The Queen of Heaven. “Although the women were primarily involved, apparently the entire family participated in some way in worshiping the ‘queen of the heavens.’ The women baked sacrificial cakes, the sons collected the firewood, and the fathers lit the fires. (Jer 7:18) That the worship of this goddess had a strong hold on the Jews is reflected by the fact that those who had fled down to Egypt after the murder of Governor Gedaliah attributed their calamity to their neglecting to make sacrificial smoke and drink offerings to the ‘queen of the heavens.’” (it "Queen of the Heavens") Insight adds that the goddess is not named in Scripture, but that the Babylonian Ishtar bore the epithets “queen of the heavens” and “queen of the heavens and of the stars,” and that in Egypt Astarte was called “lady of heaven.”

Writer: Jeremiah, writing in Egypt. These are among the last events he records; the book he began at Anathoth under Josiah (1:1, 2) is completed in exile among a people who had rejected its message (it "Tahpanes": “the remnant of Jews went down to Egypt, taking the prophet Jeremiah with them”).

What he was doing. He prayed and waited — “I am praying to Jehovah your God according to your request; and every word that Jehovah answers you, I will tell you. I will not hold back a word from you” (42:4). He delivered the answer in full, to “all the people, from the least to the greatest” (42:8), and told them to their faces that they would not obey it (42:20-22). Carried to Tahpanhes, he acted out his last recorded sign at the door of Pharaoh’s house (43:8-13) and went on preaching to the colonies up the Nile (44:1, 24).

How he coped.

  • He listened before he spoke. “The prophet listened to Johanan’s expression and then prayed to Jehovah. Later, he conveyed Jehovah’s encouraging answer, mentioning that good would come from following the divine direction to remain in the land. (Jer. 42:1-12) In both cases, Jeremiah lent a hearing ear​—he listened before he spoke.” (jr 89 ¶17)
  • He was not discouraged by refusal. “Neither Zedekiah nor Johanan accepted the encouraging advice Jeremiah delivered, and some today may not seem to respond to yours. Do not let that discourage you.” (jr 90 ¶18)
  • He kept Jehovah as his friend when his neighbours turned. “After Jerusalem was destroyed as Jeremiah had forewarned, Johanan and other military chiefs refused to heed God’s command to stay in the land of Judah. … They further defied Jehovah by taking Jeremiah and Baruch with them into Egypt.” (jr 58 ¶6)
  • He had Baruch beside him. Baruch, promised “wherever you may go, I will grant you your life as a spoil” (45:5), was accused with him (43:3) and exiled with him (43:6). Jehovah’s promise to the secretary held in Egypt as it had in the siege (it "Baruch").

Background: The people left behind by Nebuzaradan were “the poor people of the land” (40:7), reinforced by army chiefs who had escaped capture and by Jews returning from Moab, Ammon and Edom (40:11, 12). Ishmael’s murder of the Babylonian-appointed governor and of the Chaldean garrison at Mizpah (41:3) made every one of them, in their own minds, guilty by association. The parallel account is 2 Kings 25:26: “After that all the people, from small to great, including the army chiefs, rose up and went to Egypt, for they were afraid of the Chaldeans.”

Other details: Chapter 42 is built on a formal oath (42:5, 6) and its formal breach (43:4, 7); chapter 44 is a disputation — accusation (44:2-14), the people’s reply (44:15-19), Jeremiah’s rejoinder (44:20-23), and Jehovah’s verdict with a sign (44:24-30). The four Egyptian place names of 44:1 — Migdol, Tahpanhes, Noph (Memphis) and the land of Pathros — run from the frontier to Upper Egypt, so the colony had spread the length of the country. Within this guide, citations without a book name are to Jeremiah.

3.Summary

The remnant asks for Jehovah’s direction and vows to obey it. (42:1-6) Johanan, Jezaniah and “all the people, from the least to the greatest” come to Jeremiah: “pray in our behalf to Jehovah your God, … for just a few of many are left” (42:2). Jeremiah promises to hold back nothing. They swear: “Whether good or bad, we will obey the voice of Jehovah our God” (42:6).

Jehovah’s answer: stay and be built up; go to Egypt and die. (42:7-22) Ten days later Jeremiah calls them together. Remain, and “I will build you up and not tear you down” (42:10); do not fear the king of Babylon, “for I am with you, to save you” (42:11). But if they say, “No, we will go instead to the land of Egypt” (42:14), “the very sword you are afraid of will catch up with you there” (42:16). Jeremiah ends with a warning that reads their hearts: “you will not obey the voice of Jehovah your God” (42:21).

“What you are saying is a lie!” (43:1-7) Azariah, Johanan and “all the presumptuous men” answer that Jehovah has not sent him and that Baruch is inciting him to hand them to the Chaldeans (43:2, 3). Johanan and the army chiefs take “all the remnant of Judah” — men, women, children, the king’s daughters, Jeremiah and Baruch — and “went as far as Tahpanhes” (43:7).

Stones under Pharaoh’s terrace. (43:8-13) At Tahpanhes Jeremiah hides large stones in the mortar of the brick terrace at the entrance of Pharaoh’s house, with the Jewish men looking on, and declares that Nebuchadnezzar, “my servant,” will place his throne “right above these stones,” strike Egypt, burn the houses of its gods, and “wrap the land of Egypt around himself just as a shepherd wraps himself in his garment” (43:10-12).

Jehovah’s word to the Jews in Egypt. (44:1-14) Later, to the Jews of Migdol, Tahpanhes, Noph and Pathros: you have seen Jerusalem and the cities of Judah “in ruins, without an inhabitant” (44:2) because of their sacrifices to other gods, though “I kept sending all my servants the prophets … again and again” (44:4). Why repeat it in Egypt? The remnant who insisted on Egypt “will all perish in the land of Egypt” (44:12), “except for a few escapees” (44:14).

The people answer back. (44:15-19) The men who knew of their wives’ sacrifices, the women “who formed a large group,” and all the people in Pathros reply: “We will not listen” (44:16). They will keep sacrificing to the Queen of Heaven as their forefathers, kings and princes did, “when we were satisfied with bread and were well-off” (44:17); since they stopped, “we have lacked everything” (44:18). The women add that their husbands consented (44:19).

Jeremiah’s rejoinder and Jehovah’s sign. (44:20-30) It was those very sacrifices, Jeremiah says, that Jehovah remembered until “Jehovah could no longer put up with your evil practices” (44:22). Jehovah swears by his great name that it will no longer be heard in the mouth of any man of Judah in Egypt (44:26); all will perish but a few, and “all the remnant of Judah … will know whose word has come true, mine or theirs!” (44:28). The sign: “Here I am giving Pharaoh Hophra, the king of Egypt, into the hand of his enemies … just as I gave King Zedekiah of Judah into the hand of King Nebuchadnezzar” (44:30).

4.Benefit From Jeremiah 42-44

1. Draw closer to Jehovah

  1. Jehovah waited ten days and then answered in full, with a promise and a warning (42:7-22). What does the care in that answer tell you about how he regards a sincere request for guidance? (42:1-3; it "Johanan" No. 5)
  2. “I will feel regret over the calamity I have caused you” (42:10). How does it draw you to Jehovah that the God who executed the judgment is the first to want to rebuild?
  3. Jehovah gave the Jews in Egypt a sign they could check — Hophra’s fall (44:29, 30). Why does he want his worshippers to know “whose word has come true”? (44:28; Isa. 48:4, 5)

2. Deepen your understanding

The question: Jer 44:18 — Why could the Israelites not justifiably blame their suffering on the fact that they had stopped “making sacrifices to the Queen of Heaven”?

Insight answers from the way Jehovah works: “Since Jehovah gives advance warning of his judgment against stubborn ones, the execution of that judgment cannot be attributed to other causes or to a different source. Said Jehovah, through the prophet Isaiah, to obstinate Israelites: ‘Due to my knowing that you are hard and that your neck is an iron sinew and your forehead is copper, I also kept telling you from that time. Before it could come in, I caused you to hear it, that you might not say, “My own idol has done them, and my own carved image and my own molten image have commanded them.”’​—Isa 48:4, 5; compare Jer 44:16-23.” (it "Stubbornness" ¶4)

The point: the famine and the sword had been announced by name, by Jeremiah, for forty years, before they struck — and announced as the consequence of the very sacrifices the women now credited with their former prosperity (44:21-23). A judgment foretold cannot be re-labelled as a goddess’s displeasure. The women’s argument also mistook the order of events: the “good years” they remembered were the years of Josiah’s reform, when the cult was suppressed, not revived (compare 2 Ki. 23:4-14).

Why the women’s consent mattered. The women ask, “was it without the consent of our husbands …?” (44:19). Under the Law a vow made by a wife stood only if her husband did not object when he heard of it (Num. 30:6-8, 10-15). The question is a defence — our husbands approved — and an indictment: the heads of the households had let idolatry stand in their homes. Jehovah’s reply takes both at their word: “You women will surely carry out your vows and perform your vows” (44:25).

Treasures outline for the week: Before fleeing to Egypt, Johanan and the people asked Jeremiah to pray for guidance on their behalf (Jer 42:1-3; it "Johanan" No. 5) · Jehovah’s answer was clear: Stay in Judah, and do not go to Egypt (Jer 42:9, 10, 19) · The people rejected Jehovah’s direction (Jer 43:4-7). Ask yourself: ‘When I receive Bible-based counsel, do I apply it — even if it’s not what I want to hear?’ (w25.07 5 ¶14). Bible reading: Jer 43:1-13 (th study 11).

3. Apply in your life

Imitate Jehovah’s qualities.

  • Answer requests for counsel with care, not haste. Jehovah took ten days and left nothing out (42:7; 42:4). When someone asks for our help with a decision, we can take the time to pray, think and give the whole answer rather than the comfortable half of it.
  • Keep pleading with those who drift. “Sending them again and again, saying: ‘Please do not do this’” (44:4). Jehovah did not give up on idolaters at the first refusal; neither should we on a family member or a Bible student who is going the wrong way.
  • Offer to rebuild, not only to reprove. The same voice that tore down promised to “build you up … and plant you” (42:10). After correcting someone we can be the first to show that the relationship is being rebuilt.

Other lessons.

  • Ask only if you mean to listen. “Imagine that a brother has received an attractive job offer. Before he accepts it, he asks an elder for advice. … Suppose that the brother immediately finds fault with the elder’s words and asks other brothers about the matter until he is told what he wants to hear. Is the brother really seeking advice, or has he already made up his mind and is just looking for someone to agree with him? We must remember that our heart is treacherous. (Jer. 17:9) Sometimes, the advice we need the most is the advice we want to hear the least.” (w25.07 5 ¶14) Johanan had “intended” Egypt before he asked (41:17).
  • Fear makes God’s word sound like a lie. The same men who said “May Jehovah be a true and faithful witness against us” (42:5) said “What you are saying is a lie!” ten days later (43:2). Nothing had changed but the answer. When a scriptural direction frightens us, the fear, not the direction, needs examining (42:11).
  • Do not shoot the messenger’s friend. Unable to fault Jeremiah’s record, they blamed Baruch (43:3). Discrediting the person who brings counsel is an old way of escaping the counsel itself.
  • Prosperity is not proof of Jehovah’s approval — nor adversity of his absence. The women dated their good years to the cult and their bad years to its suppression (44:17, 18). Jeremiah dated the calamity to the cult itself (44:22, 23). We judge our course by Jehovah’s word, not by how things happen to be going.
  • The place of safety is where Jehovah says it is. Judah under Babylon looked like the danger and Egypt like the refuge; Jehovah said the reverse (42:10-17). Today the organization’s direction can likewise run against our instinct for self-preservation — and still be the only safe road.
  • Loyalty under compulsion. Jeremiah and Baruch were taken to Egypt against Jehovah’s stated will (43:6, 7), yet there they kept prophesying (43:8; 44:1). Being carried into a situation we did not choose does not release us from serving where we are.
