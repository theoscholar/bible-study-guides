Account overview

Lamentations 1-2 are the first two of five lyric poems that lament the destruction of Jerusalem in 607 B.C.E. They were written by the prophet Jeremiah, soon after the Babylonians broke through the city’s walls, burned the temple, and carried the people into exile. The poems do not argue a case. They grieve — and they do so in the open, before Jehovah.

Chapter 1 (22 verses) personifies Jerusalem as a widow. The city that was “full of people” now “sits all alone” (1:1). Her lovers have betrayed her, her children are in captivity, her gates lie desolate, and no one comforts her (1:2, 3, 5). Yet the poem is honest about the cause: “Jehovah has brought grief to her because of her many transgressions” (1:5), and Jerusalem herself says, “Jehovah is righteous, for it is against his commands that I have rebelled” (1:18).

Chapter 2 (22 verses) turns from the city’s grief to Jehovah’s anger. “How Jehovah has covered the daughter of Zion in the cloud of his anger!” (2:1). He has thrown down the beauty of Israel, destroyed the temple as if it were a booth in a garden, and caused festival and Sabbath to be forgotten in Zion (2:1, 6). Jeremiah weeps until his eyes are worn out (2:11), the false prophets are exposed (2:14), and the passersby mock the fallen city (2:15). The chapter closes with an appeal: “Pour out your heart like water before the face of Jehovah” (2:19).

Connection to the Bible’s theme

The Bible’s theme is the vindication of Jehovah’s sovereignty by means of his Kingdom. Lamentations 1-2 show that vindication from the side of judgment carried out — and from the side of mercy that is not yet finished.

On the side of judgment, these chapters prove that Jehovah’s word is reliable. For forty years Jeremiah had warned that Jerusalem would fall if the nation did not return to Jehovah. When the city fell, the warning was shown to be true, and the book says so plainly: “Jehovah has done what he intended; he has carried out his saying, What he commanded long ago” (2:17). The destruction vindicated Jehovah’s warnings and his justice. As All Scripture Is Inspired of God and Beneficial puts it, the book “acknowledges that Jehovah justly brought punishment upon Jerusalem and Judah because of the error of his people” (Insight on the Scriptures, Vol. 2, p. 192, “Lamentations, Book of”).

On the side of mercy, the poems never let the reader conclude that Jehovah has abandoned his people for good. Jerusalem herself confesses that Jehovah is righteous (1:18), and the book goes on to say that “it is because of Jehovah’s loyal love that we have not come to our finish, For his mercies never end. They are new each morning; your faithfulness is abundant” (3:22, 23). The same Jehovah who carried out the judgment is the one to whom the sufferers appeal — and the one who invites them back.

The Watchtower, June 1, 2007, pp. 8-11, “Highlights From the Book of Lamentations,” closes the article this way:

The Watchtower, June 1, 2007, p. 11, “Highlights From the Book of Lamentations”

“The Bible book of Lamentations reveals how Jehovah viewed Jerusalem and the land of Judah after the Babylonians burned the city and laid the land desolate. The expressions of acknowledgment of sin recorded therein make it clear that from Jehovah’s standpoint, the reason for the calamity was the error of the people. This book’s inspired songs also contain lyrics that express hope in Jehovah and the desire to turn to the right course. While these were not the sentiments of most people in Jeremiah’s day, they represented those of Jeremiah and the repentant remnant.”

That is the thread that runs through the whole Bible: Jehovah is righteous in his judgments, and he is merciful to those who return to him. Lamentations 1-2 hold both truths together in the same breath.

Keep the order straight. The poems in Lamentations 1-2 describe the destruction as it has just happened. They are not a prophecy of a future fall; they are the lament of an eyewitness standing in the ruins.

Read the chart this way. When Lamentations was completed in 607 B.C.E., most of the Hebrew Scriptures already existed — the Law, the history books through 2 Samuel, the poetry of Job, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and Song of Solomon, and the prophets Isaiah, Hosea, Joel, Amos, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk and Zephaniah. Two books sit close enough to 607 B.C.E. that they may already have been written: Obadiah (c. 607 B.C.E.) and Ezekiel (c. 591 B.C.E.). But the books of 1 and 2 Kings and Jeremiah itself were not yet completed — they were finished in 580 B.C.E., about 27 years later — and neither were Daniel, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi, Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, 1 and 2 Chronicles, or the Psalms in their final form. Lamentations is therefore one of the earliest inspired reactions to the fall of Jerusalem: it was written while the ruins were still smoking, before the history of that fall had even been written down in Kings or in Jeremiah 52.

Why are these accounts valuable?

These two chapters are valuable because they answer, honestly and without flinching, the hardest question a faithful person can face: What do you do when the worst thing you were warned about actually happens?

First, they show that Jehovah is righteous even when his judgment is severe. Jerusalem does not blame Jehovah for being unfair. She says the opposite: “Jehovah is righteous, for it is against his commands that I have rebelled” (1:18). The Watchtower, June 1, 2007, p. 10, “Highlights From the Book of Lamentations,” draws the lesson plainly: “In punishing transgressors, Jehovah is always just and righteous.” A God who punishes justly is a God we can trust — and a God whose warnings we should take seriously before it is too late.

Second, they show that Jehovah’s word comes true. The destruction was not an accident and not a surprise. Jehovah had said, through Moses and through Jeremiah, what would happen if the nation kept rebelling, and “Jehovah has done what he intended; he has carried out his saying, What he commanded long ago” (2:17). If his word of judgment proved true, his word of hope is equally sure.

Third, they give us a pattern for grieving. Jeremiah does not hide his grief, minimise it, or pretend it is over quickly. He weeps, he names the loss, and he pours it out to Jehovah (2:11, 19). That is the understandable response to a terrible loss — and it is the response the week’s Treasures talk, “An Understandable Response to a Terrible Loss,” draws attention to. Grief expressed to Jehovah is not a lack of faith. It is faith, speaking honestly.

Fourth, they hold out hope without pretending the loss did not happen. The same book that says “there proved to be no escapee or survivor” (2:22) also says, “It is because of Jehovah’s loyal love that we have not come to our finish, For his mercies never end. They are new each morning; your faithfulness is abundant” (3:22, 23). The hope is not that the destruction was unreal, but that Jehovah’s mercy outlasts it.

That is why these chapters are read in the meeting this week, and why they are worth studying slowly: they teach us to take Jehovah’s warnings seriously, to grieve honestly, and to keep hoping in his loyal love.

What to know before reading?

When. Lamentations was written in 607 B.C.E., near Jerusalem, soon after the Babylonians destroyed the city. Jeremiah is an eyewitness. He is not describing a distant event; he is standing in the ruins.

Background. For forty years Jeremiah had warned Judah that Jerusalem would fall if the nation did not return to Jehovah. The nation refused, and in 607 B.C.E. Nebuchadnezzar’s army broke through the walls after an 18-month siege, burned the temple and the city, killed the nobles, and carried the survivors into exile (Jer. 52:3-14). Lamentations is Jeremiah’s inspired response to that calamity.

The form. The Watchtower, June 1, 2007, p. 8, “Highlights From the Book of Lamentations,” explains:

The Watchtower, June 1, 2007, p. 8, “Highlights From the Book of Lamentations”

“The book of Lamentations is a collection of five lyrical poems. The first four are laments, or dirges; the fifth is a petition, or prayer. The first four songs are acrostics, successive verses beginning with a different letter in the order of the 22-character Hebrew alphabet. Although the fifth song has 22 verses to correspond to the number of letters of the Hebrew alphabet, it is not arranged alphabetically.”

So chapters 1 and 2 are each 22 verses long, and each verse begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet — a form that helped the poems to be remembered and sung. The acrostic is a memory aid, not a straitjacket: in chapter 2 the order of two letters (ayin and pe) is reversed in verses 16 and 17, because “the matter of thought content was considered more important than the adherence to a literary device that merely served as a memory aid” (The Watchtower, June 1, 2007, p. 9).

Other details. The book’s Hebrew name is its first word, ʼEh·khahʹ, meaning “How!” — the cry that opens both chapter 1 and chapter 2. The Greek Septuagint called it Threʹnoi, “Dirges; Laments.” In the Hebrew canon Lamentations stands among the five Meghil·lohthʹ (Rolls) — Song of Solomon, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes and Esther — but in our Bibles it follows Jeremiah, the book of the prophet who wrote it. Jerusalem is personified throughout as a woman — a widow, a princess, “the daughter of Zion.” When you read “she,” the “she” is the city and its people. And when Jeremiah speaks in the first person (“My eyes are worn out from shedding tears,” 2:11), the “I” is the prophet himself, weeping with his people.

Who are the main characters?
  • Jerusalem — “the daughter of Zion.” The city itself is the central figure of both poems, personified as a widow and a princess who has lost everything. She speaks, she weeps, she confesses, and she appeals to Jehovah. She is not a single person but the whole people, gathered up into one voice.
  • Jehovah. He is the one who “has brought grief to her because of her many transgressions” (1:5) and who “has covered the daughter of Zion in the cloud of his anger” (2:1) — and he is also the one to whom Jerusalem and Jeremiah appeal, the one whose loyal love and mercies never end (3:22, 23).
  • Jeremiah, the writer. He is the eyewitness and the voice of the poems. He weeps with the city (2:11), he names the false prophets (2:14), and he urges the people to pour out their hearts to Jehovah (2:19).
  • The false prophets and the priests. They “kept visioning for you false and misleading pronouncements” and “did not expose your error” (2:14). Their failure is part of why the calamity came, and part of why it was so complete.
  • The Babylonians — “the adversary” and “her enemies.” They are Jehovah’s instrument of judgment: they broke the walls, burned the temple, and carried the people into exile (1:5; 2:7-9). They are not the cause of the calamity; the cause is the nation’s transgression. The Babylonians are the means.
  • The passersby. The people of the surrounding nations who walk past the ruined city and mock it: “Is this the city about which they said, ‘It is perfect in beauty, the joy of all the earth’?” (2:15). Their scorn is itself a fulfilment of prophecy (Jer. 18:16).

Notice who is not a character here. No king speaks, no general gives orders, no single villain is blamed. The book is about a whole people and their God — and about a prophet who stands between them, grieving.

Focus character — Jerusalem, the daughter of Zion

Jerusalem is the figure these two poems are about. She is a city, but the poems treat her as a person — a widow, a princess, a mother — because she stands for the whole people of Judah, and because her suffering was the suffering of every one of them.

Jerusalem was the capital of Judah and the city Jehovah had chosen for his name. The temple stood there; the king ruled from there; the festivals were kept there. The psalmist called Mount Zion “beautiful in its loftiness, the joy of the whole earth, … The city of the Grand King” (Ps. 48:2). That is why her fall was so shattering: it was not only the loss of a city, but the loss of the centre of true worship, the place where Jehovah’s name dwelt and where his people met him.

In chapter 1 she is a widow. “How she now sits all alone, the city that was full of people! How she has become like a widow, she who was populous among the nations!” (1:1). A widow in the ancient world was a person without protection and without provision — and Jerusalem has lost her husband (Jehovah’s favour, for the moment) and her children (her sons and daughters, taken into captivity, 1:5, 18). She weeps at night and no one comforts her (1:2). Her former lovers — the nations she had courted instead of Jehovah — have betrayed her and become her enemies (1:2).

In chapter 2 she is “the daughter of Zion,” covered in the cloud of Jehovah’s anger (2:1). The beauty she was famous for has been thrown down from heaven to earth (2:1). Her walls, gates and temple are gone, and the passersby who once admired her now clap their hands and shake their heads (2:15).

But Jerusalem is not only a sufferer. She is also a confessor. In the middle of her grief she says the truest thing in the book: “Jehovah is righteous, for it is against his commands that I have rebelled” (1:18). And she is a supplicant: she calls on all who pass by to see her pain (1:12), and she is urged to “pour out your heart like water before the face of Jehovah” (2:19). That is why she is the focus of these chapters — not because she is innocent, but because she shows us what an honest response to terrible loss looks like: grief, confession, and appeal to Jehovah, all together.

There is no family tree for Jerusalem. She is a city and a people, not a person with named relatives, and the book names no individual as her “family.” Her “relatives” are the people of Judah themselves — the virgins and young men carried into captivity (1:18), the children fainting in the streets (2:11, 19), the priests and prophets (2:20).

Where did these accounts take place?

In one place: Jerusalem, in the hill country of Judah. Lamentations 1-2 name essentially no other location. There is no journey, no campaign, no flight to another city. The whole account happens inside the walls of one ruined city — and that is the point. The poems stay where the loss happened.

The places named in the poems are the parts of Jerusalem itself:

  • The city and its gates. “Her gates lie desolate” (1:4, in the older rendering; see 1:4). “Her gates have sunk down into the earth. He has destroyed and broken her bars” (2:9). The gates were where the city was entered, defended and governed; their sinking into the earth means the city can no longer protect itself or be governed.
  • The temple — Jehovah’s “footstool” and his “booth.” “He has not remembered his footstool in the day of his anger” (2:1). “He treats his booth violently, like a hut in a garden” (2:6). The “footstool” is Jehovah’s house of worship, the temple; the “booth” is the same temple, treated by the Babylonians as if it were a mere hut in a garden (Jer. 52:12, 13). The temple was the place of Jehovah’s name; its burning is the centre of the loss.
  • The streets and public squares. “The children and infants [are] fainting away in the public squares of the town” (2:11), and the children faint “at every street corner because of famine” (2:19). The famine is not in a distant field; it is in the streets where the children used to play.
  • Zion. “Zion” is Jerusalem in its sacred character — the mountain and the city where Jehovah dwelt. “The daughter of Zion” is Jerusalem as Jehovah’s own. When the poems say “Zion,” they mean the city in its relationship to God, not just its stones.

Beyond the city, only two directions are mentioned: the people have “gone into captivity” and “must dwell among the nations” (1:3, 5) — that is Babylon, to the east — and “her persecutors have overtaken her” with nowhere to rest (1:3). But Babylon itself is not described. The poems keep their eyes on Jerusalem.

Focus location — Why did Jerusalem matter?

Jerusalem mattered because it was not just a capital. It was the city Jehovah had chosen for his name, the place of his temple, and the centre of the worship of the true God on earth.

The psalmist sang of Mount Zion: “Beautiful in its loftiness, the joy of the whole earth, Is Mount Zion in the far north, The city of the Grand King” (Ps. 48:2). That praise is quoted back, bitterly, by the passersby in Lamentations 2:15: “Is this the city about which they said, ‘It is perfect in beauty, the joy of all the earth’?” The mockery only works because the praise was true. Jerusalem was perfect in beauty and the joy of all the earth — and now it is a ruin. The contrast is the wound.

Jerusalem mattered for three reasons, and the loss of each is named in the poems:

  • It was where Jehovah’s name dwelt. The temple — his “footstool” (2:1) and his “booth” (2:6) — stood there. When the temple burned, the visible centre of true worship was gone. “Jehovah has caused festival and sabbath to be forgotten in Zion, And in his fierce indignation he shows no regard for king and priest” (2:6).
  • It was where Jehovah’s law was taught. “There is no law; even her prophets find no vision from Jehovah” (2:9). The loss was not only of buildings but of instruction — the spiritual security and comfort the people had enjoyed when faithful priests and prophets taught God’s Law. (See the box “Living Without Law” in God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 6, p. 73.)
  • It was the home of Jehovah’s people. The city was “full of people” (1:1); now her children are in captivity, her virgins and young men are gone (1:18), and the children faint in the streets from famine (2:11, 19). A city is its people, and the people are scattered.

That is why the destruction of Jerusalem was not a local disaster. It was the loss of the place where heaven and earth met for God’s people — and the poems lament it as such. And that is also why the hope at the end of the book is a hope of return to Jerusalem: “Bring us back to yourself, O Jehovah, and we will readily return to you. Renew our days as in those of old” (5:21).

How was Jeremiah involved?

Jeremiah was involved in every way a prophet could be: he warned about it, he witnessed it, and he mourned it.

He warned about it for forty years. Commissioned in 647 B.C.E., Jeremiah told Judah again and again that Jerusalem would fall and the people would go into exile if they did not return to Jehovah (Jer. 1:17-19; 25:1-3, 11, 12). The nation refused. When the city fell in 607 B.C.E., “Jeremiah’s words regarding Judah and Jerusalem had come true” (God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 2, ¶24, pp. 29-30).

He witnessed it. Jeremiah was in Jerusalem during the siege and remained in the land after the fall. The poems are the work of an eyewitness: the famine in the streets (2:11, 19, 20), the gates sunk into the earth (2:9), the temple burned (2:6, 7), the children fainting (2:11). All Scripture Is Inspired of God and Beneficial notes that the vivid descriptions in chapters 2 and 4 “are evidently the pen-pictures of an eyewitness” and that the poems’ “style, phraseology, and thought … are all so characteristic of Jeremiah.”

He mourned it — and he did not say, “I told you so.” God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 3, ¶¶18-19, p. 41:

God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 3, ¶¶18-19, p. 41

“Jeremiah’s deep love for the people never cooled off. At Jerusalem’s destruction, he continued to feel compassion for them. (Read Lamentations 2:11.) The Jews had themselves to blame for their disaster. Jeremiah, however, did not say, ‘I told you so.’ Rather, he felt great sadness for what had overtaken them.”

Jeremiah is therefore not a distant reporter. He is the prophet who was right, who stayed, and who wept. That is why his lament carries weight: he grieves with the people he warned, and he grieves to Jehovah on their behalf.

How did he cope?

Jeremiah coped the way the poems themselves show: by telling Jehovah the truth about what had happened, by confessing the sin that caused it, and by holding on to Jehovah’s loyal love.

He named the grief. He did not minimise it or hurry past it. “My eyes are worn out from shedding tears. My insides are churning. My liver has been poured out on the ground, because of the downfall of the daughter of my people” (2:11). The poems give the grief words, verse after verse, in the acrostic form — a discipline that let the grief be fully said and fully remembered.

He confessed the cause. “Jehovah is righteous, for it is against his commands that I have rebelled” (1:18). Jeremiah does not blame Jehovah, and he does not pretend the people were innocent. Naming the sin honestly is part of coping, because it leaves the door open to forgiveness.

He poured out his heart to Jehovah. The poems turn, again and again, from describing the ruin to addressing Jehovah directly: “See, O Jehovah, and look upon the one with whom you have dealt so harshly” (2:20). And the counsel of chapter 2 is to do the same: “Rise up! Cry out during the night, at the start of the watches. Pour out your heart like water before the face of Jehovah” (2:19).

He held on to Jehovah’s loyal love. The book’s centre — chapter 3 — is the turn from grief to hope: “It is because of Jehovah’s loyal love that we have not come to our finish, For his mercies never end. They are new each morning; your faithfulness is abundant” (3:22, 23). “For Jehovah will not cast us off forever. Although he has caused grief, he will also show mercy according to his abundant loyal love. For it is not in his heart to afflict or grieve the sons of men” (3:31-33). And the book’s closing prayer is a request to return: “Bring us back to yourself, O Jehovah, and we will readily return to you. Renew our days as in those of old” (5:21).

Jeremiah’s coping, then, was not to get over the loss quickly. It was to grieve honestly before Jehovah, to confess honestly, and to keep appealing to the God whose mercies are new each morning. That is the pattern the book offers to anyone who suffers a terrible loss.

Key accounts

Chapter 1 — the widow. Jerusalem sits alone like a widow (1:1). She weeps at night and no one comforts her; her lovers have betrayed her (1:2). Judah has gone into exile and finds no resting-place (1:3). “Jehovah has brought grief to her because of her many transgressions. Her children have gone into captivity before the adversary” (1:5). “Jerusalem has sinned greatly. That is why she has become something abhorrent” (1:8). She calls to all who pass by: “Is there any pain like the pain that was dealt out to me, Which Jehovah made me suffer in the day of his burning anger?” (1:12). And she confesses: “Jehovah is righteous, for it is against his commands that I have rebelled. Listen, all you peoples, and see my pain. My virgins and my young men have gone into captivity” (1:18).

Jeremiah’s eyes are worn out from tears (2:11). The prophets’ visions were false and empty; they did not expose the error (2:14). The passersby clap their hands and mock: “Is this the city about which they said, ‘It is perfect in beauty, the joy of all the earth’?” (2:15). “Jehovah has done what he intended; he has carried out his saying, What he commanded long ago” (2:17). Then the turn: “Rise up! Cry out during the night… Pour out your heart like water before the face of Jehovah” (2:19). And the hardest question in the book: “Should women keep eating their own offspring, their own fully formed children, Or should priests and prophets be killed in the sanctuary of Jehovah?” (2:20).

Connections to other Bible books
  • Jeremiah. Lamentations is the sequel to Jeremiah’s prophecies and the companion to Jeremiah 52. The fall the book laments is the fall Jeremiah 52 records (Jer. 52:3-14), and the prophecies it fulfils are the ones Jeremiah spoke for forty years. Jeremiah is also the writer of Lamentations (God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 1, ¶2, p. 4). The two books belong together: Jeremiah warns, Lamentations grieves, and both point to the same God.
  • Leviticus. The “saying” Jehovah carried out (2:17) is Leviticus 26:17 — the covenant warning that if Israel disobeyed, Jehovah would set his face against them and they would be defeated by their enemies. See “Prophecy fulfilled.”
  • Deuteronomy. The famine horror of chapter 2 — women eating their own offspring (2:20) — is the curse Moses foretold in Deuteronomy 28:53 for a nation that would not listen to Jehovah. The Watchtower, June 1, 2007, p. 10: “The Israelites were warned that if they did not listen to the voice of Jehovah, they would experience maledictions, which included eating ‘the flesh of their sons and daughters.’ (Deuteronomy 28:15, 45, 53) How unwise to choose a course of disobedience to God!”
  • Psalms. The praise the passersby quote — “perfect in beauty, the joy of all the earth” (2:15) — echoes Psalm 48:2, “Beautiful in its loftiness, the joy of the whole earth, Is Mount Zion in the far north, The city of the Grand King.” And the “footstool” of 2:1 is Jehovah’s house of worship, as Psalm 132:7 says: “Let us come into his grand tabernacle; let us bow down at his footstool.”
  • 1 and 2 Kings. The history of the fall — the siege, the breach, Zedekiah’s flight and capture, the burning of the temple — is told in 2 Kings 24:18–25:30, largely parallel to Jeremiah 52. Lamentations is the inspired, poetic response to that history.
  • Ezekiel. Ezekiel was a contemporary of Jeremiah, prophesying to the exiles in Babylon while Jeremiah remained in the land. His visions of the fall (Ezek. 7, 24) and his message that Jehovah is an exact timekeeper (Ezek. 4:4-6) run alongside Lamentations; both books end in 607 B.C.E.
  • Daniel. Daniel 9:5-14 corroborates Lamentations 1:5 and 3:42: Daniel, praying in exile, confesses the same sin and the same justice of Jehovah that Lamentations confesses.
  • The Christian Greek Scriptures. The book’s lessons are applied in the Christian congregation. The apostle Paul’s words, “each of us will render an account for himself to God” (Rom. 14:12), are the principle behind the counsel not to carry blame for another’s choices (The Watchtower, August 2023, pp. 29-30, ¶¶12-13). And the lost son’s father, who “ran and embraced him and tenderly kissed him” (Luke 15:20), is the picture of Jehovah’s initiative toward the repentant that the week’s Treasures talk draws from Lamentations’ hope (The Watchtower, August 2024, p. 28, ¶¶7-9).

Draw closer to Jehovah

Lamentations 1-2 draw us closer to Jehovah by showing us two sides of him at once — and by refusing to let go of either one.

He is righteous. “Jehovah is righteous, for it is against his commands that I have rebelled” (1:18). A God who punishes justly is not a God to fear in the sense of dreading arbitrary cruelty. He is a God whose judgments are right, whose warnings are true, and whose word can be trusted. When we see that he did what he said he would do — even when it was terrible — we can trust that he will do what he has said he will do for the future: bring the Kingdom, end suffering, and restore all things.

He is merciful, and his mercy outlasts his anger. “It is because of Jehovah’s loyal love that we have not come to our finish, For his mercies never end. They are new each morning; your faithfulness is abundant” (3:22, 23). “For Jehovah will not cast us off forever. Although he has caused grief, he will also show mercy according to his abundant loyal love. For it is not in his heart to afflict or grieve the sons of men” (3:31-33). The God who judged Jerusalem is the same God who invites her back — and who took the initiative to reach out to his wayward people “even before they showed any signs of repentance” (The Watchtower, August 2024, p. 28, ¶7).

He listens when we pour out our hearts. “Rise up! Cry out during the night… Pour out your heart like water before the face of Jehovah” (2:19). Jehovah is not a God who wants us to hide our grief or to pretend we are not hurting. He invites the honest cry. The book itself is proof: Jehovah inspired a book that is almost entirely a cry of grief, and he preserved it for us to read. That tells us what he thinks of honest prayer.

Draw closer, then, by doing what Jerusalem and Jeremiah did: tell Jehovah the truth about your loss, confess honestly where you have fallen short, and keep appealing to his loyal love — which is new every morning.

Deepen your understanding

Why is the book an acrostic? Chapters 1, 2 and 4 each have 22 verses, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, in order; chapter 3 has 66 verses, three for each letter. The form is a memory aid — it helped the poems to be learned, sung and passed on. But the content mattered more than the form: in chapter 2 the letters ayin and pe are reversed in verses 16 and 17, and the same reversal appears in chapters 3 and 4. The inspired writers “did not do so at the cost of sounding artificial or unnatural. The matter of thought content was considered more important than the adherence to a literary device that merely served as a memory aid” (The Watchtower, June 1, 2007, p. 9).

What is the “footstool” and the “booth”? “He has not remembered his footstool in the day of his anger” (2:1). The “footstool” is Jehovah’s house of worship, the temple (Ps. 132:7). “He treats his booth violently, like a hut in a garden” (2:6): the Babylonians burned the house of Jehovah as if it were a mere hut in a garden (Jer. 52:12, 13). Both words point to the same loss — the temple, treated with contempt.

What does “there is no law” mean? “Her king and her princes are among the nations. There is no law; even her prophets find no vision from Jehovah” (2:9). Jeremiah did not mean the survivors had become an uncontrollable mob. He meant the loss of the spiritual security and comfort the Jews had enjoyed when faithful priests and prophets instructed them in God’s Law — and the false prophets who had their attention were offering no real “vision,” or direction, from Jehovah. (See the box “Living Without Law,” God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 6, p. 73.)

What is the “winepress”? “Jehovah has trodden the winepress of the virgin daughter of Judah” (1:15, in the older rendering). In destroying the city, the Babylonians shed blood in such quantity that it was comparable to the squeezing of grapes in a winepress. Jehovah foretold this and allowed it to happen, so it can be said that he had “trodden the winepress” (The Watchtower, June 1, 2007, p. 9).

Study questions.

  • Why is Jerusalem personified as a widow in chapter 1? What does that picture tell us about her loss? (1:1, 2, 5)
  • How does Lamentations 1:18 answer the charge that Jehovah was unfair to punish Jerusalem?
  • What is Jehovah’s “footstool,” and what happened to it? (2:1, 6; Ps. 132:7)
  • Why did the false prophets make the calamity worse? (2:14)
  • How was “the beauty of Israel thrown down from heaven to earth”? (2:1; The Watchtower, June 1, 2007, p. 9, ¶1)
  • What “saying” did Jehovah carry out, and where was it spoken beforehand? (2:17; Lev. 26:17)
  • What does Lamentations 2:19 urge the sufferers to do, and when?
Apply in your life

Take Jehovah’s warnings seriously — now, not later. Jerusalem’s tragedy is that the warning was given for forty years and was not heeded. “How unwise to choose a course of disobedience to God!” (The Watchtower, June 1, 2007, p. 10). The fruitage of transgression “is not joy; it is tears, sighing, grief, and bitterness” (same article, p. 10). When Jehovah warns us — through his Word, through the congregation, through our conscience — the wise course is to listen and adjust while there is still time.

Grieve honestly, and pour it out to Jehovah. If you suffer a terrible loss — the death of someone you love, the collapse of a marriage, a child who leaves the truth, a serious illness — do what Jeremiah and Jerusalem did: name the loss, weep, and “pour out your heart like water before the face of Jehovah” (2:19). Grief expressed to Jehovah is not a lack of faith. It is faith. Do not let anyone tell you that a faithful person should not grieve, or should get over it quickly. Lamentations is an entire inspired book of grief, and Jehovah preserved it for us.

Do not carry blame that is not yours. A son or daughter may decide to stop serving Jehovah, and that can cause untold grief to the parents. But “parents who blame themselves for their child’s poor decision take on a heavy burden. It is not a weight that Jehovah expects them to bear” (The Watchtower, August 2023, p. 29, ¶12). “Jehovah has given all of us free will. … The choice your child makes is his responsibility, not yours” (p. 30, ¶13). And “if your child makes any effort to return to Jehovah, He will eagerly receive him back” (p. 30, ¶13; Luke 15:18-20).

Take the initiative toward the repentant — as Jehovah does. “Jehovah did not wait for his wayward people in ancient Israel to take the first step. Rather, he took the initiative to reach out to them even before they showed any signs of repentance” (The Watchtower, August 2024, p. 28, ¶7). Like the father of the lost son, who “ran and embraced him and tenderly kissed him” (Luke 15:20), Jehovah does not wait for the wrongdoer to beg. If someone in your life has strayed and shows a sign of return, do not make it difficult for them to come back. Elders, in particular, “genuinely want the wrongdoer to return, and they do not make it difficult for him to do so” (p. 28, ¶7).

Be compassionate, not “I told you so.” Jeremiah was right about everything, and he never said, “I told you so.” He “felt great sadness for what had overtaken them” (God’s Word for Us Through Jeremiah, ch. 3, ¶18, p. 41). When someone suffers the consequences of a bad decision, our ministry and our friendship should prove how much we love them — not how right we were.
